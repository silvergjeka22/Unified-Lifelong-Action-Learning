\begin{abstract}
We present \emph{Unified Lifelong Action Learning} (ULAL), an end-to-end pipeline
that studies how a single video action recogniser can keep learning new actions over
time without forgetting the old ones, trained directly on real video frames with no
cached features. The pipeline is built as five linked studies, each answering one
question. (i) \emph{How do we measure honestly?} We show that UCF101's shipped split
leaks 80\% of its source groups across train and test and replace it with a
group-disjoint split that leaks none. (ii) \emph{Which backbone?} A five-way bake-off
selects a ResNet50+LSTM teacher (93.9\% on the base classes). (iii) \emph{How do we
stop forgetting?} Across a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ class stream, rehearsal reaches 89.8\%
while plain fine-tuning collapses to 26.0\% and every data-free regulariser (EWC, LwF)
trails. (iv) \emph{How do we shrink the model?} Knowledge distillation compresses the
teacher into a MobileNetV3-Small student that keeps 82.7\% at roughly $14\times$ fewer
parameters, with cosine feature distillation best. (v) \emph{How do we learn brand-new
classes from a handful of clips?} A nine-arm meta-learning comparison, controlled by a
matched fine-tune baseline that sees the same auxiliary data, shows \emph{MAML+replay}
is the best-balanced few-shot adapter (harmonic mean 0.60) and beats the fine-tune
control by 0.07, isolating the meta-learning effect. For every stage we first explain
\emph{why} the methods should rank as they do from the ability each one has, then show
the measured result. A final capstone notebook chains the winning choice of every stage
and is described here pending its run. The whole pipeline runs on a single free-tier
Colab T4 GPU.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Deep video models forget. Fine-tuning a trained action recogniser on a set of new
classes collapses its accuracy on the classes it already knew---\emph{catastrophic
forgetting}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. Lifelong, or continual, learning aims to add
classes over time while preserving what was already learned~\cite{delange2021survey}.
Video makes this markedly harder than images: a clip is a large spatio-temporal tensor,
so storing and replaying past data is expensive, and the temporal model on top of the
per-frame features adds a second place for representations to drift.

A realistic deployment does not need only one of these abilities; it needs several at
once. It must (a) be measured on data it has never seen, (b) start from strong features,
(c) retain old classes as new ones arrive, (d) be small enough to run on a modest
device, and (e) still pick up a brand-new class from only a handful of examples. Each of
these is usually studied in isolation. The contribution of this paper is to study them
\emph{together}, as one pipeline on one dataset, trained \emph{directly on real video
frames} so that every reported number reflects the model that would actually be
deployed---no offline feature cache stands between the study and the recogniser.

We build the pipeline on UCF101~\cite{soomro2012ucf101} as five linked studies, each a
single notebook answering one question, and each feeding the next. The organising idea
is a \emph{moving freeze boundary}: the recogniser is one shared network (a CNN backbone
applied per frame, an LSTM over the frame features, and a classifier head that
\emph{grows} as classes arrive), and each stage decides how much of that network stays
trainable. Early stages train broadly; later stages freeze the lower backbone to protect
old features and let only the upper blocks, the LSTM, and the head move. This single
design connects all five studies and lets each comparison change exactly one mechanism
at a time.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An honest measurement protocol.} We quantify the 80\% source-group leakage
in UCF101's shipped split and replace it with a group-disjoint split that leaks nothing,
so every later number is trustworthy though lower.
\item \textbf{A controlled, five-stage study} of backbone selection, five
continual-learning methods, four distillation objectives, and a nine-arm meta-learning
comparison, all sharing one recogniser and one hyperparameter budget per stage.
\item \textbf{A fine-tune control that isolates meta-learning.} In the few-shot stage the
MAML init is compared against an ordinary fine-tune init that saw the \emph{same}
auxiliary data, so any remaining gain is attributable to meta-learning and not to extra
data.
\item \textbf{A balanced metric for the few-shot regime.} Because base classes outnumber
new ones, we report the harmonic mean of base and new accuracy, which reveals a ranking
that a base-dominated average hides.
\item \textbf{Explanations, not just tables.} For every stage we state \emph{why} one
method should beat another from its ability before showing the measured result, and we
close with a cross-stage synthesis of when each tool wins.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Continual learning.} Approaches fall into three families.
\emph{Regularisation} methods add a penalty that discourages changing parameters
important to old tasks: EWC~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} weights the penalty by the Fisher
information, and synaptic-intelligence variants use online importance
estimates~\cite{zenke2017si}. \emph{Functional} methods keep the model's outputs stable:
Learning without Forgetting (LwF)~\cite{li2017lwf} distils the old model's own
predictions on new data. \emph{Rehearsal} methods store and replay a small set of past
exemplars; iCaRL~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl} combines rehearsal with distillation and a
nearest-mean classifier. We evaluate a representative of each family on video, where the
cost of storing exemplars is a first-order concern. \textbf{Knowledge distillation.} A
compact student can be trained to match a larger teacher's softened
logits~\cite{hinton2015kd} or its intermediate features~\cite{romero2015fitnets}; we
compare logit distillation against two feature objectives (cosine and a
regressor-MSE) following the structure of the PyTorch distillation tutorial.
\textbf{Meta and few-shot learning.} MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} learns an initialisation
from which a few gradient steps adapt to a new task; first-order MAML drops the
second-order term for efficiency. Few-shot class-incremental learning
(FSCIL)~\cite{tao2020fscil} adds new classes from few examples while retaining a large
base set, and it is standard there to report the harmonic mean of base and new accuracy
so that neither side can be ignored. Our few-shot stage sits in this setting and adopts
that metric.

\section{System, Notation, and Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The shared recogniser}
Every stage uses the same recogniser, sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. A clip is
$T{=}16$ RGB frames at $224\times224$, ImageNet-normalised. A CNN backbone $\phi$ is
applied to each frame independently, producing a sequence of frame features
$\phi(x_1),\dots,\phi(x_T)$. An LSTM reads that sequence and its final hidden state
$h_T\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$ is the clip embedding. A linear head $W$ maps $h_T$ to class
logits. Formally, for a clip $x$,
\begin{equation}
h_T = \mathrm{LSTM}\big(\phi(x_1),\dots,\phi(x_T)\big),\qquad
z = W\,h_T .
\end{equation}
The head \emph{grows} as classes arrive: adding $k$ classes appends $k$ rows to $W$ and
keeps the existing rows, so old-class logits are never discarded, only joined by new
ones. We call the shared clip embedding $h_T=\varphi(x)$ the model's \emph{feature}; it
is what distillation aligns and what the prototype probe reads.

\subsection{The moving freeze boundary}
The five stages differ in \emph{which} parameters they train, and this is the thread that
ties the pipeline together. Stage~2 trains the whole recogniser to obtain strong base
features. Stage~3 keeps most of the backbone plastic but only the last block is
fine-tuned as classes are added, so that low-level features stay put. Stage~4 trains a
separate small student end-to-end to match the teacher. Stage~5 freezes the lower
backbone and meta-trains only the upper blocks, the LSTM, and the head, so the old-class
features are preserved while the upper layers reshape for the new task. Making this
boundary explicit is what lets each comparison be fair: within a stage every method
trains the \emph{same} parameters, so only the mechanism under test differs.

\subsection{Datasets and budget}
All experiments use UCF101 on a single free-tier Colab T4 GPU. The group-disjoint split
assigns whole source groups to a single split in an $18/3/4$ groups-per-class ratio
(train/val/test), preserving the standard $\approx$72/12/16\% proportions while removing
leakage (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Table~\ref{tab:hparams} lists the per-stage
hyperparameters; every method compared within a stage shares them, and seeds are fixed
and reset before each training run, so a difference in the result comes only from the
mechanism under test.

\subsection{How to read the numbers}
\emph{Test accuracy} is the fraction correct on the unseen, group-disjoint test set.
\emph{Old} and \emph{New} accuracy split that score into classes learned earlier versus
the newest task, and \emph{Base} is retention on the original classes. In the few-shot
stage, where 20 base classes outnumber 5 new ones, a plain average is $20/25$ dominated
by the base and rewards a method that simply guards old classes while learning nothing
new. We therefore add the \emph{balanced} mean
$\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}+\mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}})$ and the
\emph{harmonic} mean
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:harm}
\mathrm{H}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}\,\mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}}}
{\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}+\mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}}},
\end{equation}
which weight the two sides equally and, for the harmonic, collapse to zero if either side
is sacrificed. This is the standard FSCIL metric~\cite{tao2020fscil}.

\section{Stage 1 --- Data Split and Leakage}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Why this comes first.} A UCF101 filename
\texttt{v\_Class\_gXX\_cYY.avi} encodes a \emph{group} \texttt{gXX} (one source
recording) and a \emph{clip} \texttt{cYY} (one segment of that recording). All clips of a
group share nearly identical frames---the same actor, background, and camera. If the
split is made per clip, segments of the \emph{same} recording land in both train and
test, and the model is scored on footage it effectively trained on: it can recognise the
\emph{scene} rather than the \emph{action} and appear far stronger than it is. No later
comparison can be trusted until this is fixed, so we assign each whole group to a single
split.

\textbf{Result.} Across the 20 studied classes (2{,}679 videos, 500 groups), the shipped
split leaks \textbf{400 of 500 groups (80\%)} across train/val/test, whereas the
group-disjoint split leaks \textbf{0} (Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage}). The disjoint split keeps
the conventional $\approx$72/12/16\% proportions, so the drop in accuracy that follows is
purely the removal of an inflation, not a change of task difficulty.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\figph{leakage.png}{[leakage.png] shipped vs group-disjoint leakage bar.}
\caption{Source groups that leak across the splits: 80\% under the shipped per-clip split
versus 0\% under the group-disjoint split. Every subsequent number in this paper is read

\label{fig:leakage}

\textbf{Reading it.} The lesson is methodological rather than architectural: a dataset's
default split can silently reward memorising context. Because the same leakage would
inflate every stage below---backbone, continual learning, distillation, and
meta-adaptation alike---fixing it once at the source is what makes the rest of the study
comparable to itself and to future work.

\section{Stage 2 --- Backbone Selection}
\label{sec:backbone}
\textbf{Why we expect differences.} The backbone supplies the per-frame features; the
LSTM only has to combine them over time. A network with no prior knowledge (a
from-scratch CNN) must learn good spatial features from a few thousand clips and should
struggle, whereas ImageNet-pretrained backbones already carry rich features and should
cluster high. Among the pretrained backbones the choice becomes a trade-off of accuracy,
feature dimensionality, and speed, so we compare five and select the best balance as the
teacher for every later stage.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:backbone} gives the measured numbers. The from-scratch
CNN is far behind (52.8\%); all four pretrained backbones exceed 90\%. \textbf{ResNet50
is both the most accurate (93.9\%) and a good size/speed balance}, so it becomes the
teacher.

\textbf{Why the ordering.} The from-scratch CNN has no prior features \emph{and} its LSTM
ingests a large flattened feature map, giving it the most parameters (103M) yet the worst
accuracy: capacity without useful priors does not help on little data. The pretrained
backbones all reach the low-90s because the hard part---good spatial features---is
already solved before the LSTM sees anything. ResNet50 edges the others thanks to its
deeper, richer 2048-d feature, which also hands the later distillation and meta stages a
clean feature space to build on; its 55~ms/clip is acceptable and less than half of
VGG19-BN's 116~ms. We therefore trade a slightly larger parameter count for the best
accuracy and the best downstream feature.

\section{Stage 3 --- Continual Learning}
\label{sec:cl}
\textbf{The five methods and why they should differ.} Starting from the base teacher we
grow the head and train on a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ class stream with five methods. Their
abilities predict the outcome. \emph{Naive} fine-tuning minimises only the new-task loss,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{naive}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{new}),
\end{equation}
so it has no mechanism to protect the past and should forget badly. \emph{EWC} adds a
quadratic penalty that resists moving parameters important to old tasks,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{new})
+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i(\theta_i-\theta_i^{\star})^2,
\end{equation}
where $F_i$ is the Fisher importance and $\theta^\star$ the old weights; it stores no data
and can only \emph{slow} drift. \emph{LwF} keeps the old-class outputs stable by distilling
the old model's predictions on the new data,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LwF}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{new})
+\lambda\,\mathrm{KD}\big(z^{\text{old}}_{1:C_{\text{old}}},\,z^{\star}_{1:C_{\text{old}}}\big),
\end{equation}
a stronger signal than a weight penalty but still without ever seeing old data.
\emph{Replay} re-exposes the model to a few stored old clips $\mathcal{R}$ mixed into each
new batch $\mathcal{B}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{replay}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{R}),
\end{equation}
directly refreshing the old decision boundaries. \emph{Replay+LwF}~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}
adds output stability on top of re-exposure. All arms share the schedule of
Table~\ref{tab:hparams}; only the mechanism differs.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:cl} reports accuracy after the \emph{full} 20-class
stream, split into old classes (0--14) and the newest task (15--19), plus base (0--9)
retention. Figure~\ref{fig:forget} contrasts naive forgetting with replay across the
whole stream.

\textbf{Why the winners win.} The table follows exactly from the abilities. \emph{Naive}
and \emph{LwF} learn the newest task almost perfectly (94.9\% and 100\%) but erase
everything older (old $\le5\%$): with the shared weights free to move and no old data, the
network re-purposes itself entirely for the last task. LwF even fits the new task
\emph{better} than naive because its distillation term stabilises optimisation, yet it
still forgets---keeping \emph{outputs} stable is no substitute for \emph{seeing} old data.
\emph{EWC} is the opposite failure: its penalty protects the base (76.9\%) but is so
restrictive that the new task barely learns (36.8\%), a stability/plasticity lock. Both
\emph{replay} arms win decisively (89.8\% and 87.0\%) because re-exposing even 15 clips
per class refreshes the old boundaries \emph{while} the new task is learned. Notably plain
replay slightly beats replay+LwF here: the buffer already protects the old classes, so the
extra LwF term adds a mild drag on new-class learning without a retention gain. The lesson
for video is blunt---\emph{storing a little data beats any data-free regulariser}---and it
is why the capstone uses replay.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 4 --- Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:kd}
\textbf{Why and how.} The retained model is a 25.9M-parameter ResNet50 teacher; for
deployment we want something far smaller. We distil it into a MobileNetV3-Small+LSTM
student ($\sim$1.8M parameters, $\sim$14$\times$ smaller) and compare four objectives, all
of the form $\mathcal{L}=\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+(1-\alpha)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}$
with $\alpha=0.75$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CE only:} $\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}=0$ (labels alone, the baseline).
\item \textbf{KD}~\cite{hinton2015kd}: match softened logits,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}=T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\sigma(z_t/T)\,\|\,\sigma(z_s/T)\big)$.
\item \textbf{Cosine:} align the 256-d LSTM features by direction,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}=1-\cos\!\big(\varphi_s(x),\varphi_t(x)\big)$.
\item \textbf{MSE-regressor}~\cite{romero2015fitnets}: a learnable regressor $g$ maps the
student feature onto the teacher's before an $\ell_2$ match,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}=\lVert g(\varphi_s(x))-\varphi_t(x)\rVert_2^2$
(the regressor is dropped at inference).
\end{itemize}
We expect the student to recover most of the teacher's accuracy---its knowledge
transfers---but the gap between objectives to be small, because the student backbone is
itself pretrained and already strong.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:kd}: the student keeps \textbf{82.7\%} against the
teacher's 87.0\% at 14$\times$ fewer parameters, with cosine feature distillation the best
of the four objectives.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Distillation into the MobileNetV3-Small student (20-class group-disjoint test).
All students share one budget, seed, and initialisation; only the transfer term differs.}
\label{tab:kd}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Model & Test acc.\ (\%) & Params (M) \\
\midrule
Teacher (Replay+LwF, ResNet50) & 87.0 & 25.9 \\
Student, CE only               & 80.6 & 1.8 \\
Student, CE + KD               & 79.7 & 1.8 \\
\textbf{Student, CE + cosine}  & \textbf{82.7} & 1.8 \\
Student, CE + MSE (regressor)  & 75.9 & 1.8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why.} Compression works because the teacher's decision function transfers: a
14$\times$ smaller student lands within $\sim$4 points of the teacher. Among objectives,
\emph{cosine} on the LSTM state wins because it aligns the \emph{direction} of the
student's temporal feature with the teacher's without forcing its exact magnitude---a
gentle, achievable target across two different backbones. Plain \emph{KD} on logits helps
least here: the student is pretrained, so labels already carry it far and the extra ``dark
knowledge'' in softened logits is small. \emph{MSE} is the harshest objective (it demands
matching direction \emph{and} magnitude between mismatched feature bases) and does worst.
The honest headline of this stage is therefore \emph{compression}---teacher-level
behaviour at 14$\times$ fewer parameters---with a modest bonus from feature-cosine
distillation, exactly as the ability of each objective predicts.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 5 --- Few-Shot Meta-Adaptation}
\label{sec:meta}
\textbf{The question and the design.} Given the deployed 20-class student, how well can it
learn \emph{brand-new} classes from only a handful of clips? We keep the 20 base classes
and add 5 new sports (a 5-way task, matching the 5-way meta-training). Every method uses
the \emph{same} few-shot adaptation---the same 8 support clips per class, the same 5
epochs, learning rate, seed, and the same replay buffer / LwF teacher objects---so only
two things vary: the \emph{initialisation} and whether replay / LwF are switched on. Three
inits (\emph{plain} student; \emph{fine-tune}, i.e.\ the student after ordinary supervised
training on 30 auxiliary sports; and \emph{MAML}, the student after meta-training on the
same 30 sports) are crossed with three protection settings, giving nine arms. The
\emph{fine-tune} arm is the key control: it saw the same extra data as MAML but trained in
the ordinary way, so \emph{MAML vs fine-tune} isolates meta-learning from ``just more
data.''

\textbf{How MAML shapes the init.} First-order MAML learns an initialisation $\theta$ from
which a few gradient steps reach a good task-specific solution. For each episode it adapts
on a support set,
\begin{equation}
\theta' = \theta - \alpha\,\nabla_\theta\,\mathcal{L}^{\text{sup}}(\theta),
\end{equation}
then updates the initialisation by the loss of the \emph{adapted} weights on a held-out
query set,
\begin{equation}
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \beta\,\nabla_{\theta'}\,\mathcal{L}^{\text{qry}}(\theta'),
\end{equation}
(first order: the gradient is taken at $\theta'$). Over many episodes $\theta$ moves to a
place from which one task is a few steps away. To keep this on a free T4 and to protect the
base features, only the last backbone blocks, the LSTM, and the head are meta-trained; the
lower backbone stays frozen. Because meta-training shifts the features, we then re-fit a
fresh linear head to the shifted features (a short linear probe) so that the MAML init is
placed on equal footing with the plain init, whose head already matches its features.

\textbf{Why the metric.} With 20 base and 5 new classes, a plain overall accuracy is
$20/25$ base and rewards a method that guards the base and learns nothing new. We rank by
the harmonic mean of Eq.~\eqref{eq:harm}, which weights the two sides equally and collapses
to zero if either is sacrificed.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:meta} (measured; base before adapting $=0.759$) ranks the
nine arms. \textbf{MAML+replay is best}, reaching a balanced \textbf{0.60}, and it
\emph{beats fine-tune+replay by 0.07 harmonic}, so the gain is really from meta-learning
and not merely from the extra data.
Figures~\ref{fig:metacompare}--\ref{fig:oldnew} show the per-method comparison, the
shot-count trend, the meta-training curve, and a readable old-vs-new confusion.

re-exposure protects the old classes but the un-primed init learns the new sports slowly.

project as a whole, replay is the reliable retention tool and MAML is the tool reserved
for the genuinely few-shot extension.

\subsection{Cross-stage synthesis}
Read together, the five stages tell one story about \emph{regimes}. Measurement dominates
everything: the group-disjoint split lowers but validates every number, and in Stage~5 the
harmonic mean surfaces a MAML+replay win that a base-dominated average hides. For
retention on video, \emph{data beats cleverness}: rehearsing a few clips outperforms every
data-free regulariser, because the shared weights will always drift toward the newest task
unless old data pulls them back. For deployment, distillation's win is \emph{compression},
not a new objective---the gap between transfer losses is small once the student is
pretrained. And meta-learning's win is \emph{few-shot extension}, largest when only one or
two clips are available and vanishing as data grows. Each tool has a regime, and the
pipeline is the claim that, chained in the right order, their abilities compose.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The ULAL Capstone (to be run)}
\label{sec:ulal}
The final notebook, \texttt{ULAL.ipynb}, chains the winning choice of every stage into one
lifelong pipeline and is evaluated on real video, including clips outside UCF101.
\emph{This section describes the intended run; the numbers below are expectations from the
per-stage studies, not fresh measurements.}

\textbf{Stage by stage.} (1) Train the ResNet50+LSTM teacher on the base classes (the
Stage-2 winner). (2) Grow it across a two-task $10\!\to\!20\!\to\!30$ stream with
\emph{replay}, the winning retention ability from Stage~3, printing the per-task accuracy,
the old-vs-new split, the confusion matrix, and the forgetting curve exactly as in the
continual-learning study. (3) Clear the GPU and distil the grown teacher into the
MobileNet student with the \emph{cosine} objective (the Stage-4 winner), reporting the
accuracy kept and the compression ratio. (4) Clear the GPU again and meta-train the compact
student with MAML. (5) Test on real clips downloaded from the web: for \emph{known} classes
this measures domain robustness (test the student directly and report accuracy and a
confusion matrix); for \emph{new} classes it measures learn-from-few (expand the head and
few-shot adapt, expecting accuracy to rise from near zero after only a few examples), with
a dedicated small-shot sweep over 1, 3, and 5 real clips per class.

\textbf{Why this order.} Each stage supplies an ability the next one needs---strong
features, data-rich retention, deployable size, fast few-shot extension. The capstone is
the concrete claim that these abilities \emph{compose} into a single model that is
accurate, small, retains old classes, and still picks up new ones from little data. Based
on the per-stage results we expect the student to keep most of its known-class accuracy on
real clips and to improve markedly on new classes after few-shot adaptation, mirroring the
Stage-5 shot sweep. The measured numbers will be filled in once the run completes; the
real-video test depends on downloadable clips, which is the capstone's main external risk.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Three themes recur across the pipeline. \emph{Measurement matters:} without the
group-disjoint split every later number would be inflated by scene memorisation, and
without the harmonic mean the few-shot ranking would be wrong. \emph{Data beats cleverness
for retention:} on video, rehearsal of a few clips outperforms every data-free regulariser
we tried (EWC, LwF), and even a small buffer makes the difference between 26\% and 90\%
final accuracy. \emph{Each tool has a regime:} distillation's win is compression, and
meta-learning's win is few-shot extension---asked outside their regime (for example MAML
with plenty of data per class) they tie simpler baselines, which is itself an honest and
useful finding.

\textbf{Limitations.} The study uses a single dataset and short streams (up to $20{+}5$
classes); scaling to the full 101 classes and to longer streams is future work. Rehearsal
stores real clips, so its retention is not free in memory; generative or feature-level
rehearsal would remove that cost. Meta-learning's advantage is confined to the few-shot
regime, and the small student caps the achievable new-class accuracy. Finally, the capstone
run and its real-video test are still pending and depend on the availability of external
clips.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
ULAL is a compact, honest, real-video pipeline for lifelong action recognition on a single
free GPU. A leakage-free split makes the numbers trustworthy; a ResNet50+LSTM is a strong
teacher (93.9\%); rehearsal is the strongest continual learner (89.8\% versus 26.0\% for
naive fine-tuning); distillation yields a 14$\times$ smaller student that keeps 82.7\%; and,
scored fairly with a harmonic metric and a fine-tune control, MAML+replay is the
best-balanced few-shot adapter (0.60 harmonic). The organising idea---one shared recogniser
with a moving freeze boundary---lets each stage change exactly one mechanism and lets their
abilities compose. The capstone assembles the best of every stage into one lifelong model
and is left to a final run.
