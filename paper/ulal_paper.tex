\begin{abstract}
We present \emph{Unified Lifelong Action Learning} (ULAL), an end-to-end pipeline
for continual learning of a video action recogniser on UCF101, trained directly on
video frames. The pipeline (i) builds an honest, group-disjoint data split that
removes the leakage present in the shipped per-clip split, (ii) selects a
ResNet50+LSTM backbone, (iii) extends it to new classes with five continual-learning
strategies (naive, EWC, LwF, replay, and replay+LwF), (iv) compresses the best model
into a MobileNet student through knowledge distillation, and (v) adapts the student to
a shifted (YouTube) domain, comparing meta-learning (Reptile) against plain
fine-tuning under an active-learning budget. We describe the design, the honest
evaluation protocol, and the trade-offs of each stage. The marks \pa, \pb, \pc\ denote
the author responsible for each part.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
continual learning, catastrophic forgetting, action recognition, knowledge
distillation, meta-learning, domain adaptation.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Deep video models forget: fine-tuning a trained recogniser on new action classes
collapses accuracy on the old ones, a failure known as \emph{catastrophic
forgetting}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. Lifelong (continual) learning aims to add
classes over time while retaining past knowledge. Video makes this harder than
images: clips are large, temporal, and expensive to store and process.

This paper describes a single, reproducible pipeline that studies the problem on
UCF101~\cite{soomro2012ucf101} while training \emph{directly on real video frames} --
no cached features -- so that the reported numbers reflect the deployed model. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item An honest data split. UCF101's shipped train/val/test split mixes clips of the
same source video across splits; we quantify the leakage and replace it with a
group-disjoint split (\pa).
\item A staged pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) that chains backbone selection,
continual learning, distillation, and domain adaptation, with a consistent evaluation
protocol (\pa, \pb, \pc).
\item A controlled comparison of five continual-learning strategies, of four
distillation objectives, and of meta vs.\ plain adaptation under an equal budget
(\pb, \pc).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Continual learning.} Regularisation methods such as
EWC~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} penalise changes to weights important for old tasks.
Distillation methods such as LwF~\cite{li2017lwf} keep the old-class outputs stable
using the model's own past predictions. Rehearsal stores a few exemplars and replays
them; iCaRL~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl} combines replay with distillation. We evaluate all
of these on video.

\textbf{Knowledge distillation.} A compact student can be trained to match a larger
teacher's softened logits~\cite{hinton2015kd} or intermediate features. We compare
logit and feature (cosine, MSE) objectives.

\textbf{Meta-learning and adaptation.} Reptile~\cite{nichol2018reptile} is a
first-order meta-learning rule that adapts quickly from few examples. We use it to
adapt to a shifted domain and compare it with plain fine-tuning.

\section{Method}
The model is a CNN backbone applied per frame, an LSTM over the 16 frames of a clip,
and a linear classifier. The classifier grows as new classes arrive; the backbone is
fine-tuned only in its last block. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the five stages.

\subsection{Data split and leakage (\pa)}
A UCF101 file \texttt{v\_Class\_gXX\_cYY.avi} encodes a \emph{group} \texttt{gXX} (one
source video) and a \emph{clip} \texttt{cYY} (one segment of it). Clips of the same
group are near-duplicate frames. The shipped split is made per clip, so segments of one
recording land in both train and test; the model is then tested on footage it trained
on, and every accuracy is inflated. We measure the fraction of groups that span more
than one split and rebuild a \emph{group-disjoint} split that assigns each whole group
to a single split, giving honest -- and lower -- numbers.

\subsection{Backbone selection (\pa)}
Five backbones (a from-scratch CNN, ResNet18, ResNet50, DenseNet121, VGG19-BN), each
with an LSTM head, are trained on the base classes and compared on accuracy, size, and
per-clip latency. ResNet50+LSTM is chosen as the teacher for its balance of accuracy,
size, and speed.

\subsection{Continual learning (\pb)}
Starting from the base teacher, the head is expanded for each task and the model is
trained with one of five strategies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Naive}: plain fine-tuning (the lower bound).
\item \textbf{EWC}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}: a Fisher-weighted penalty holds
important weights near their old values; no stored data.
\item \textbf{LwF}~\cite{li2017lwf}: a frozen pre-task copy supplies soft targets for
the old-class logits; no stored data.
\item \textbf{Replay}: a small buffer of past clips is mixed into every batch.
\item \textbf{Replay+LwF}~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}: both together.
\end{itemize}
All arms share the same schedule and differ only in the mechanism, so they are directly
comparable. We report the per-task accuracy matrix, average accuracy, and forgetting.

\subsection{Distillation (\pb)}
The best continual model (replay+LwF) is the teacher for a MobileNetV3-Small+LSTM
student trained on all classes. We compare four objectives: cross-entropy only (CE),
CE+KD (softened logits~\cite{hinton2015kd}), and CE with cosine or MSE distance between
the 256-d LSTM states of teacher and student.

\subsection{Domain adaptation (\pc)}
The student is adapted to real YouTube clips of some base classes, which differ from
UCF101 in compression, lighting, and framing. Predictive-entropy active learning
selects a small labelled budget, and the student is adapted two ways from the same
start and the same budget: plain fine-tuning and Reptile~\cite{nichol2018reptile}
meta-adaptation. We report target accuracy, source retention, and wall-clock time.

\section{Experimental Setup}
UCF101 subset of 20 classes as a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ stream (the capstone uses the
full $10\!\to\!20\!\to\!30$). Clips are 16 frames at $224\times224$, ImageNet
normalised. The group-disjoint split uses 18/3/4 groups per class for train/val/test.
Backbones freeze all but the last block; the LSTM hidden size is 256. For a fair
comparison, \emph{all continual-learning arms share the same optimizer, learning
rate, and number of epochs} -- only the mechanism and its own strength knob differ:
EWC's penalty weight $\lambda$, LwF's distillation weight and temperature, and the
replay budget (15 clips per class). We deliberately avoid freezing extra layers or
lowering the learning rate for any single arm, since either would change its trainable
capacity or step size and confound the result. Distillation uses temperature
$T\!=\!2$ with weights $0.75/0.25$; Reptile uses a meta step $\epsilon\!=\!0.2$.
Seeds are fixed for reproducibility.

\section{Conclusion}
ULAL is a compact, honest, real-video pipeline for lifelong action recognition. A
leakage-free split makes the numbers trustworthy; replay+LwF is the strongest
continual-learning arm; distillation yields a much smaller student at a small accuracy
cost; and meta-adaptation narrows a real domain gap under a tiny labelling budget.
Future work includes longer task streams, more backbones, and generative rehearsal.
