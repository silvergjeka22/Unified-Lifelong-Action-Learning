\begin{abstract}
We present \emph{Unified Lifelong Action Learning} (ULAL), an end-to-end pipeline
that studies how a video action recogniser can keep learning new actions over time
without forgetting the old ones, trained directly on real video frames (no cached
features). The pipeline (i) builds an honest, group-disjoint data split that removes
the leakage in UCF101's shipped split, (ii) selects a ResNet50+LSTM backbone from a
five-way bake-off, (iii) extends it to new classes with five continual-learning
strategies (naive, EWC, LwF, replay, replay+LwF), (iv) compresses the best model into
a MobileNetV3-Small student with four distillation objectives, and (v) adapts the
student to \emph{new, few-shot} classes with first-order MAML, using a matched
fine-tune baseline so the meta-learning effect is isolated. For each stage we report
the measured results and, crucially, \emph{explain why} they come out the way they do
from the abilities of each method. A final capstone notebook (ULAL) chains the best
choice of every stage into one pipeline and is evaluated on real video; it is
described here and left to a final run. Our headline findings: a leakage-free split
lowers but trusts the numbers; rehearsal (replay+LwF) is by far the strongest
continual learner; distillation buys roughly $20\times$ compression at a small
accuracy cost; and meta-learning helps specifically in the few-shot regime, where
\emph{MAML+replay} is the best-balanced adapter once accuracy is scored with a metric
that does not let the many old classes drown out the few new ones.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
continual learning, catastrophic forgetting, action recognition, knowledge
distillation, meta-learning, few-shot learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Deep video models forget. Fine-tuning a trained recogniser on new action classes
collapses its accuracy on the old ones, a failure called \emph{catastrophic
forgetting}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. Lifelong (continual) learning aims to add
classes over time while retaining what was already learned. Video makes this harder
than images: clips are large and temporal, so storing and replaying past data is
expensive, and the model is bigger and slower to adapt.

This paper describes a single, reproducible pipeline on UCF101~\cite{soomro2012ucf101}
that trains \emph{directly on real video frames}---no cached embeddings---so every
number reflects the model you would actually deploy. Each stage is one notebook, and
each stage answers one question: \emph{how do we measure honestly? which backbone?
how do we stop forgetting? how do we shrink the model? and how do we learn brand-new
classes from only a handful of clips?} Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An honest split.} UCF101's shipped split mixes clips of the same source
video across train and test; we quantify the leakage and replace it with a
group-disjoint split, giving lower but trustworthy numbers.
\item \textbf{A staged, controlled comparison} of five continual-learning methods,
four distillation objectives, and a nine-arm meta-learning study whose fine-tune
baseline isolates the meta-learning effect.
\item \textbf{An explanation, not just a table.} For every stage we tie the result to
the \emph{ability} of each method, so the ordering of the numbers is understandable
rather than reported.
\end{itemize}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Continual learning.} Regularisation methods such as EWC~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}
penalise changes to weights important for old tasks. Distillation methods such as
LwF~\cite{li2017lwf} keep old-class outputs stable using the model's own past
predictions. Rehearsal stores and replays a few exemplars; iCaRL~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}
combines replay with distillation. We evaluate all of these on video.

\textbf{Knowledge distillation.} A compact student can be trained to match a larger
teacher's softened logits~\cite{hinton2015kd} or its intermediate features. We compare
logit and feature (cosine, MSE) objectives, following the structure of the standard
distillation tutorial.

\textbf{Meta- and few-shot learning.} MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} learns an
initialisation that adapts quickly from few examples; it is the basis of our stage-5
study. Because the base classes greatly outnumber the new ones, we report the
harmonic mean of base and new accuracy, the standard metric in few-shot
class-incremental learning.

\section{Method and Model}
The recogniser is a CNN backbone applied to each of the 16 frames of a clip, an LSTM
over those frame features, and a linear classifier on the final LSTM state. The
classifier \emph{grows} as new classes arrive (new rows are appended, old rows kept),
and only the last block of the backbone is fine-tuned, which keeps the number of
trainable parameters modest. Clips are 16 frames at $224\times224$, ImageNet
normalised. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the five stages, each described below with
its methods, its measured results, and an explanation of \emph{why} the results look
as they do.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All stages train on 16-frame clips at $224\times224$, ImageNet-normalised, on a single
GPU (Google Colab T4). The group-disjoint split uses 18/3/4 groups per class for
train/val/test. Each pretrained backbone freezes all but its last block; the LSTM
hidden size is 256, dropout $0.4$. Stage 3 uses a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ class stream
(base 10, then two 5-class tasks); Stage 4 distils the resulting 20-class model; Stage
5 treats that 20-class student as the base and adds 5 new sports. Within each stage,
every compared method shares the settings in Table~\ref{tab:hparams}, so any
difference in the results comes only from the mechanism under test---this is what makes
each comparison fair. Seeds are fixed and reset before every training call.

\subsection{How we read the numbers}
Five simple metrics recur in the paper. \emph{Test accuracy} is the fraction of clips
classified correctly on the unseen (group-disjoint) test set. \emph{Average accuracy}
(AA) is the mean accuracy over all classes seen so far, the headline number for a
continual learner. \emph{Forgetting} is how much a past task's accuracy has dropped
from its best value---high forgetting means the model overwrote old knowledge.
For the few-shot stage, where 20 old classes outnumber 5 new ones, a plain average is
dominated by the old classes, so we add the \emph{balanced} mean
$(\text{acc}_\text{new}+\text{acc}_\text{base})/2$ and the \emph{harmonic} mean, which
both weight new and old equally and, in the harmonic case, punish sacrificing either
side. Reporting these makes a method that learns new classes visible, instead of one
that simply guards the many old ones winning by default.

\section{Stage 1 --- Data Split and Leakage}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Method.} A UCF101 file \texttt{v\_Class\_gXX\_cYY.avi} encodes a \emph{group}
\texttt{gXX} (one source video) and a \emph{clip} \texttt{cYY} (one segment of it).
All clips of one group are near-duplicate frames. The shipped split is made per clip,
so segments of one recording land in both train and test. We measure the fraction of
groups that span more than one split, then rebuild a \emph{group-disjoint} split that
assigns each whole group to a single split (18/3/4 groups per class for
train/val/test).

\textbf{Result.} A large fraction of groups leak across splits in the shipped data;
the group-disjoint split removes the leak entirely (Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage}). The
practical effect is that every downstream accuracy \emph{drops}, because the model can
no longer be tested on footage it trained on.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\figph{figures/leakage.png}{[Figure: data leakage]\\Export from
1\_dataset/dataset\_study.ipynb: \texttt{plot\_split\_leakage(ALL\_CLASSES\_LIST)}
(shipped vs group-disjoint groups that leak).}
\caption{Groups (source videos) that leak across train/val/test in the shipped split
versus the group-disjoint split. Leakage in the shipped split inflates all
downstream numbers.}
\label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why.} A model can memorise a scene rather than the action. If a test clip
comes from a video the model already saw in training, it recognises the \emph{scene}
and scores high for the wrong reason. Splitting by whole group makes the test set
genuinely unseen, so the numbers become honest---and lower. Every result in the rest
of the paper is read against this clean split.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 2 --- Backbone Selection}
\label{sec:backbone}
\textbf{Methods.} Five per-frame backbones, each with an LSTM head, are trained on the
base classes and compared on accuracy and size: a from-scratch CNN, ResNet18,
ResNet50, DenseNet121, and VGG19-BN. All but the last block of each pretrained
backbone is frozen.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:backbone} shows the trade-off. The from-scratch CNN is
far behind; all ImageNet-pretrained backbones are strong; ResNet50 is chosen as the
teacher for its balance of accuracy, a well-understood feature space, and manageable
size.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Backbone comparison on the base classes (group-disjoint test).}
\label{tab:backbone}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Backbone & Test acc.\ (\%) & Params (M) \\
\midrule
ScratchCNN        & 59.0 & 8.5 \\
ResNet18          & 89.6 & 11.7 \\
\textbf{ResNet50 (teacher)} & \textbf{92.9} & 25.9 \\
DenseNet121       & 94.3 & 8.5 \\
VGG19-BN          & 93.9 & 20.4 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why.} The from-scratch CNN has no prior knowledge, so with a few thousand
clips it cannot learn good spatial features---hence 59\%. The pretrained backbones
already carry rich ImageNet features, so the LSTM only has to learn temporal
combination, and they cluster in the low-90s. DenseNet is marginally most accurate at
fewer parameters, but ResNet50 gives a clean, widely-used 2048-d feature that the
later distillation and meta stages build on; the small accuracy gap is a deliberate
trade for a simpler downstream pipeline.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 3 --- Continual Learning}
\label{sec:cl}
\textbf{Methods.} Starting from the base teacher, the head is expanded for each task
and the model is trained on a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ class stream with one of five
strategies. \emph{All arms share the same optimizer, learning rate, epochs, and
seed}; only the mechanism (and its own strength knob) differs, so the comparison is
fair.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Naive}: plain fine-tuning; the lower bound.
\item \textbf{EWC}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}: a Fisher-weighted penalty holds
important weights near their old values; stores no data.
\item \textbf{LwF}~\cite{li2017lwf}: a frozen pre-task copy supplies soft targets for
the old-class logits; stores no data.
\item \textbf{Replay}: a small buffer of past clips (15 per class) is mixed into every
batch.
\item \textbf{Replay+LwF}~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}: rehearsal and distillation together.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:cl} reports average accuracy (AA) over all classes
seen and mean forgetting after the full stream, against a joint-training ceiling.
Figure~\ref{fig:forget} shows each task's accuracy across the whole stream.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Continual learning after the $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ stream. AA: average
accuracy; Forget: mean drop on old tasks; Mem: stored clips per class.}
\label{tab:cl}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Method & AA (\%) & Forget (\%) & Mem \\
\midrule
Naive             & 29.2 & 91.1 & 0 \\
EWC               & 35.2 & 81.9 & 0 \\
LwF               & 44.0 & 68.1 & 0 \\
Replay            & 70.4 & 26.8 & 15 \\
\textbf{Replay+LwF} & \textbf{77.4} & \textbf{13.1} & 15 \\
\midrule
Joint (ceiling)   & 80.2 & -- & all \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\figph{figures/forgetting.png}{[Figure: forgetting curve]\\Export from a

\texttt{plot\_forgetting\_history(forget\_hist, cfg.CL\_EPOCHS)}.}
\caption{Per-task test accuracy across the whole stream. Naive's old-task lines
collapse when a new task is learned; replay+LwF holds every line high.}
\label{fig:forget}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why (per ability).} The ordering follows exactly from what each method can do.
\emph{Naive} has no memory ability at all: the shared weights are freely overwritten
to fit the new task, so old-class accuracy is destroyed (forgetting 91\%). \emph{EWC}
can only slow weight change through a penalty; because video features shift a lot
between action groups, the penalty is not enough to protect the old decision
boundaries, giving a modest gain. \emph{LwF} keeps the old-class \emph{outputs} stable
using the model's own past predictions, which is a stronger signal than a weight
penalty, but with no old data it still drifts. \emph{Replay} re-exposes the model to
actual old clips, directly refreshing the old classes---this is why it jumps to 70\%.
\emph{Replay+LwF} adds output-stability on top of re-exposure, combining the two
abilities, and reaches 77\%, within three points of the joint ceiling. The lesson is
that \emph{storing a little data beats any data-free regulariser} on video.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 4 --- Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:kd}
\textbf{Methods.} The best continual model (replay+LwF, a ResNet50 teacher) is
distilled into a MobileNetV3-Small+LSTM student trained on all classes. We compare
four objectives from the standard distillation tutorial: cross-entropy only (CE),
CE+KD (softened logits~\cite{hinton2015kd}), and CE with a cosine or a
regressor-MSE loss between the 256-d LSTM states of teacher and student.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:kd} shows the student recovers most of the teacher's
accuracy at roughly $20\times$ fewer parameters. The four objectives are close;
soft-target KD gives a small edge over CE alone.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Distillation into the MobileNetV3-Small student (20-class test).}
\label{tab:kd}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Model & Test acc.\ (\%) & Params (M) \\
\midrule
Teacher (replay+LwF, ResNet50) & 77.4 & 25.9 \\
Student, CE only               & 71.5 & $\approx$1.5 \\
Student, CE + KD               & \textbf{72.5} & $\approx$1.5 \\
Student, CE + cosine           & 71.5 & $\approx$1.5 \\
Student, CE + MSE (regressor)  & 72.4 & $\approx$1.5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why.} The compression works because the teacher's decision function transfers:
the student, guided by the teacher, reaches teacher-level behaviour with a fraction of
the weights. The reason KD only \emph{slightly} beats CE is the student backbone is
itself ImageNet-pretrained and therefore already strong; the labels alone get it most
of the way, so the extra ``dark knowledge'' in the soft targets adds only a small
amount. The feature objectives (cosine, MSE) are similar because they push the
student's 256-d state toward the teacher's, which helps a little but is constrained by
the different backbones. The honest headline is therefore \emph{compression}
(teacher-level accuracy at $\sim$20$\times$ fewer parameters), not ``KD $\gg$ CE'',
which only appears when the student is weak or data is scarce.

\section{Stage 5 --- Few-Shot Meta-Adaptation}
\label{sec:meta}
\textbf{Setup and methods.} Stage 5 asks a different question: given the deployed
student that already knows 20 classes, how well can it learn \emph{brand-new} classes
from only a handful of clips? We keep 20 base classes and add 5 new held-out sports
(a 5-way task, matching the 5-way episodes used in meta-training). Every method uses
the \emph{same} few-shot adaptation---the same support clips, the same epochs, the
same learning rate, the same seed, and the same replay buffer / LwF teacher---so the
only differences are the \emph{initialisation} and whether replay/LwF are on. Three
initialisations are crossed with three protection settings, giving nine arms:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Init:} \emph{plain} (the student as-is), \emph{fine-tune} (the student
after ordinary supervised training on 30 auxiliary sports), and \emph{MAML} (the
student after first-order MAML meta-training~\cite{finn2017maml} on those same 30
sports).
\item \textbf{Protection:} none, \emph{replay}, or \emph{replay+LwF}.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{fine-tune} init is the key control: it saw exactly the same extra data as
MAML but trained in the ordinary way, so comparing MAML to fine-tune isolates the
meta-learning effect from the effect of merely seeing more data.

\textbf{Metric.} Because 20 base classes vastly outnumber 5 new ones, a plain overall
accuracy is dominated by the base ($20/25$ of the weight) and rewards a method that
simply guards the base while learning nothing new. We therefore report the
\emph{harmonic mean} of base and new accuracy (the standard few-shot
class-incremental metric), which weights the two equally and punishes sacrificing
either:
\begin{equation}
\text{harmonic} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{acc}_{\text{new}} \cdot \text{acc}_{\text{base}}}
{\text{acc}_{\text{new}} + \text{acc}_{\text{base}}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:meta} reports new-class accuracy, base retention, the
balanced mean, and the harmonic mean, ranked by harmonic. \emph{MAML+replay} is the
best-balanced method; the meta init lifts new-class accuracy while replay protects the
base. Figures~\ref{fig:metabar}--\ref{fig:oldnew} show the per-method comparison and a
readable old-vs-new confusion.

\textbf{Why (per ability).} Each row of Table~\ref{tab:meta} follows from what the
arm can do. \emph{MAML alone} has the best new-class accuracy but its base retention
is zero: a meta-learned initialisation is built to move its features quickly onto the
new task, and with no protection it moves them all the way, erasing the old classes.
\emph{Replay alone} does the opposite: it protects the base by re-exposure but starts
from an init that was never primed for the new sports, so its new-class accuracy is
lower. \emph{MAML+replay} combines the two abilities---a fast-adapting init plus
re-exposure---and therefore tops the harmonic ranking. The \emph{fine-tune} arms are
the honest control: because they saw the same 30 sports, \emph{fine-tune+replay} is a
strong baseline, but \emph{MAML+replay} still beats it, which shows the gain comes
specifically from meta-learning and not merely from the extra data. Finally, the
LwF variants over-protect: with only 5 few-shot clips, distilling the old outputs
crushes the fresh new-class head, so their new-class accuracy is lowest.

\textbf{When meta-learning helps.} MAML's advantage is a property of the \emph{init},
so it is largest when adaptation is short and data is scarce (few clips, few epochs).
As the number of support clips or epochs grows, ordinary fine-tuning reaches the same
solution and the meta advantage shrinks (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}, left). This is why we
report the few-shot regime: it is the setting where the meta init genuinely pays off.
Figure~\ref{fig:curves} (right) also shows that meta-training itself is learning---the
meta query accuracy rises across epochs, confirming the init is being shaped rather
than left at the student's starting point.

\section{The ULAL Capstone (to be run)}
\label{sec:ulal}
The final notebook, \texttt{ULAL.ipynb}, chains the best choice of every stage into a
single lifelong pipeline and is evaluated on real video. \emph{This section describes
the intended pipeline; the final run is pending and the numbers below are
expectations, not measurements.}

\textbf{What happens, stage by stage.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Base teacher.} Train ResNet50+LSTM on the base classes---the strongest,
best-understood backbone from Stage 2.
\item \textbf{Continual learning (replay).} Grow the model across the class stream
with rehearsal, the winning ability from Stage 3, so the data-rich old classes are
retained.
\item \textbf{Distillation.} Compress the grown teacher into the MobileNetV3-Small
student (Stage 4), keeping most of the accuracy at $\sim$20$\times$ fewer parameters
for deployment.
\item \textbf{Few-shot MAML.} Meta-train the compact student on auxiliary novel
classes, then adapt it to brand-new classes from a handful of clips (Stage 5), the
regime where meta-learning helps.
\item \textbf{Real-video test.} Evaluate on real clips: for \emph{known} classes this
measures domain robustness (test the student directly); for \emph{new} classes it
measures learn-from-few (expand the head and few-shot adapt, expecting accuracy to
rise from near zero after only a few examples).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why this order.} Each stage supplies an ability the next one needs: the
backbone gives strong features; rehearsal gives data-rich retention; distillation
gives a deployable size; and meta-learning gives fast few-shot extension. The capstone
is the argument that these abilities \emph{compose}---a single model that is accurate,
small, retains old classes, and can still pick up new ones from little data. We expect
the student to keep most of its known-class accuracy on real clips and to improve
markedly on the new classes after few-shot adaptation, mirroring Stage 5. The measured
numbers will be added once the run completes.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion}
Three themes run through the results. First, \emph{measurement matters}: the
group-disjoint split lowers every number but makes them trustworthy, and in Stage 5 the
harmonic mean is what reveals MAML+replay's win that a base-dominated average hides.
Second, \emph{data beats cleverness for retention}: on video, rehearsal (even a few
clips per class) outperforms every data-free regulariser, and combining it with
distillation is best. Third, \emph{each tool has a regime}: distillation's win is
compression, and meta-learning's win is few-shot extension; asked outside their regime
(e.g.\ MAML with plentiful data) they tie simpler baselines. Reading each method by
its ability, rather than by a single leaderboard number, is what makes the pipeline
coherent.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Limitations}
Several limits should be read alongside the results. (i) \emph{One dataset and short
streams}: we study UCF101 with streams up to $20$ base $+\,5$ new; longer streams and
more datasets would test the conclusions further. (ii) \emph{Rehearsal has a memory
cost}: replay and replay+LwF store clips (15 per class in Stage 3, 8 per class in Stage
5), so their strong retention is not free---generative rehearsal is a natural next step
to remove the buffer. (iii) \emph{Meta-learning is regime-specific}: MAML's advantage
holds in the few-shot, few-epoch setting and disappears when data is plentiful, so it
complements rather than replaces rehearsal. (iv) \emph{Small student}: the
MobileNetV3-Small student caps the achievable few-shot accuracy on new classes; a
larger student would raise the absolute numbers at the cost of the compression story.
(v) \emph{The capstone's real-video test} depends on downloadable clips and is
sensitive to source quality; its numbers are pending a final run.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
ULAL is a compact, honest, real-video pipeline for lifelong action recognition. A
leakage-free split makes the numbers trustworthy; replay+LwF is the strongest
continual learner; distillation yields a much smaller student at a small accuracy
cost; and, scored fairly, MAML+replay is the best-balanced few-shot adapter, with a
fine-tune control confirming the gain is from meta-learning. The capstone composes the
best of each stage and is left to a final run. Future work includes longer streams,
larger new-class sets, and generative rehearsal to remove stored exemplars.
