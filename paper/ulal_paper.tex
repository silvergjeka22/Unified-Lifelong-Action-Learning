\begin{abstract}

We present \emph{Unified Lifelong Action Learning} (ULAL), an end-to-end pipeline that illustrates how one 
can construct and train a powerful video action recogniser, extend its knowledge over time to new classes 
without forgetting old ones, adapting to new scenarios, compress it into a lighter model mitigating performance
degradation, and learn new classes under a few-shot regime. The proposed pipeline is composed of five linked 
studies plus a reference study. Each module answers one of the following questions, comparing
several methodologies aiming to find the most suitable one for the current framework. 
(i) \emph{How do we avoid data leakage?} UCF101's native train-test split leaks 80\% of its data across train and test,
meaning that different frames from the same video clip are present in both sets. This setting pollutes training, 
as train and test sets must be completely disjoint. We replace this mechanism with a new
group-disjoint split that avoids leakage completely. 
(ii) \emph{Which backbone should we select?} To select the most suitable backbone (i.e., feature extractor) for the pipeline, 
we compare four ImageNet-pretrained CNNs (ResNet18, ResNet50, DenseNet121, and VGG19-BN) and a vision
transformer (ViT-B/16), all of which are fine-tuned on the current dataset, against a light, custom CNN
trained from scratch. All architectures are attached a LSTM module to capture temporal dependencies. Through this comparison,
we select a ResNet50+LSTM model (93.9\% on the base classes): the vision transformer is marginally more accurate
(94.3\%, one more correct test clip) but has $3.4\times$ more parameters and a $3.4\times$ longer inference time.
On the native split the same backbones score 4.3 to 36.7 points higher, and the vision transformer reaches 99.4\%,
showing how much leakage inflates the results.
(iii) \emph{How do we prevent forgetting?} To prevent forgetting on a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ class stream, we tested several 
continual learning strategies, including reharsal, EWC, and LwF. The best-performing method, rehearsal, obtaines an overall accuracy of 89.8\%,
while naive fine-tuning on new classes collapses to 26.0\%. This demonstrates the effectiveness of continual learning approaches when extending knowledge of a
model. 
(iv) \emph{How do we shrink the model?} To transfer knowledge from the large ResNet50+LSTM teacher to a smaller MobileNetV3-Small+LSTM student, 
we compare three distillation objectives: logits alignment, features alignment (both through cosine similarity and MSE), 
and a new class-prototype alignment objective, which extracts and aligns class prototypes instead of the whole feature maps.
Under full-data regime, cosine feature distillation allows the MobileNetV3-Small student
to achieve an accuracy of 82.7\%, at $14\times$ fewer parameters than the teacher model; with only 32 training clips per class, 
the new class-prototype alignment objective raises the student's accuracy from 61.5\% to 67.9\%. 
(v) \emph{How do we learn new classes from a few clips?}
To teach the distilled student five new sports from only eight clips each, we compare three starting points
(the plain student, a student fine-tuned on 40 other sports, and a student meta-trained with MAML on the same
sports), each adapted with no protection, with replay, or with replay and LwF. Scored with the harmonic mean of
new- and old-class accuracy, MAML combined with replay is the best-balanced strategy (0.600), ahead of the
fine-tuned control with replay (0.530), which saw the same extra data; with one clip per new sport, MAML already
reaches 0.26 new-class accuracy, against at most 0.03 for the other starts.
(vi) \emph{How can we exploit frozen foundation models?} Vision-language models such as CLIP have been shown to be effective
under few-shot regimes. As a reference for Stage~5, we compare our ResNet50+LSTM against a frozen CLIP encoder with a small
MLP adapter, using 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 training samples per class. With one sample per class, the CLIP+MLP architecture
achieves an accuracy of 75.5\%, against 17.9\% for our ResNet50+LSTM. However, the CLIP encoder is $50\times$ larger than
the distilled student and solves a different task, so these results are not directly comparable with Stage~5.
To test the generalisation abilities of our approach, a final capstone combines the best strategy of each stage into a
single pipeline and evaluates it on real YouTube videos of five unseen sports, using 10 clips per class for training.
Under small update budgets the meta-trained student adapts faster than the plain distilled student: after 13 updates
it reaches a new-class accuracy of 0.16 on the unseen test videos, while the plain student reaches 0.00, and after 26
updates it reaches 0.36, compared with 0.23 for the plain student and 0.37 for the $14\times$ larger teacher. With 65
updates the plain student catches up and surpasses it (0.57 against 0.47), confirming that meta-learning is most useful
when adaptation must be fast.
The whole pipeline runs on a single free Colab T4 GPU.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Deep video models are prone to forget old classes when fine-tuned on a set of new ones, as models' parameters for old knowledge get overwritten. This phenomenon is know as {catastrophic
forgetting}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. 
Continual (or lifelong) learning aims to mitigate this issue, extending current knowledge to new classes while preserving what was already learned~\cite{delange2021survey}. 
Videos make this remarbably harder than images: a clip is a large spatio-temporal tensor, and therefore storing and replaying past data is expensive, and the temporal model on top of the
per-frame spatial feature extractor adds a second layer subjected to representation drift.

A realistic deployment demands several capabilities at once. A model must (a) be
evaluated on data it has never seen, (b) begin with strong features, (c) retain existing
classes as new ones arrive, (d) remain compact enough to run on modest hardware, and (e) be able to learn a new class from only a handful of examples. Although these capabilities are
typically studied in isolation, we examine them \emph{together} within a single pipeline
and on a common dataset. The models are trained \emph{directly on real video frames}, so
each reported result reflects the model that would ultimately be deployed.

Our pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) is built on UCF101~\cite{soomro2012ucf101} and it is composed of five
linked studies (S1--S5), each of them answering one of the proposed questions by comparing different strategies. 
We include a sixth study (S6), which compares meta-learning with a
different route to few-shot learning: a frozen CLIP foundation model used as backbone.

Our base method follows a \emph{moving freeze boundary} strategy: it is composed of a CNN backbone used to extract spatial features from each frame, of a LSTM used to extract temporal dependencies, and and a classifier head that \emph{grows} as classes arrive.

\textbf{Main Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fair measurement protocol.} We quantify the 80\% source-group leakage in UCF101's native train-test split and replace it with a group-disjoint split that avoids leakage.
\item \textbf{A comprehnsive study for each stage}: we evaluate six backbones (including a vision transformer) to select the best spatial feature extractor, five continual-learning
strategies, four distillation objectives, and nine meta-adaptation arms.
\item \textbf{A fine-tune control that isolates meta-learning}: we compare the MAML starting point with an ordinary
fine-tuned starting point trained on the \emph{same} extra sports, so that the gain attributed to meta-learning is not
simply due to the additional data.
\item \textbf{A foundation model reference}: we include in our study a few-shot learning strategy leveraging a frozen CLIP encoder against our ResNet50+LSTM trained on the same few clips.
\item \textbf{A real-video capstone}: the best strategy selected at each stage is inserted into a single
pipeline, which is evaluated over five unseen sports from a few YouTube clips.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Continual learning (CL).} Traditional CL approaches mainly fall into three families.
\emph{Regularisation methods} add a penalty to the standard loss function that discourages changing parameters recognized to be
important to the old tasks: EWC~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} weights the penalty by the Fisher information, while synaptic-intelligence variants use online importance estimates~\cite{zenke2017si}. 
\emph{Functional methods} try to keep the model's outputs stable: for example, Learning without Forgetting (LwF)~\cite{li2017lwf} distils the old model's own predictions on new data. \emph{Rehearsal methods} store a portion of old samples and use them during training, along with new samples for new tasks. One can also combine different strategies: iCaRL~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl} combines rehearsal with distillation. 
\textbf{Knowledge distillation (KD).} Traditional KD approaches try to inject into a compact model (student) the knowledge of larger one (teacher) by matching their softened logits~\cite{hinton2015kd} or their intermediate features~\cite{romero2015fitnets}. 
\textbf{Meta and few-shot learning.} Few-shot learning aims to recognise new classes from only a few labelled
examples. Metric-based approaches such as prototypical networks~\cite{snell2017proto} classify each sample according to
its distance from class prototypes in an embedding space. Optimisation-based approaches such as
MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} instead learn an initialisation that can be adapted to a new task with only a few gradient
steps. First-order MAML improves efficiency by omitting second-order derivatives. Few-shot class-incremental learning
(FSCIL)~\cite{tao2020fscil} extends this setting by requiring a model to learn new classes from few examples while
retaining its base-class knowledge. We evaluate this balance using the harmonic mean of base- and new-class accuracy,
which penalises poor performance on either group. Finally, large vision-language models such as
CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} provide general-purpose features that support new-class recognition with little or no
additional training; we use CLIP as a reference in Stage~6.

\section{System, Notation, and Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The shared architecture}
We process each video clip to obtain $T{=}16$ RGB frames
at $224\times224$ resolution, ImageNet-normalised.
Once the best recognizer is established, it is used for every stage of the study (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). This model is composed of a CNN-backbone for spatial feature extraction, a LSTM module for temporal dependencies learning, and a final classification head.
The CNN-backbone $\phi$ is applied to each frame
independently, producing a sequence of spatial features $\phi(x_1),\dots,\phi(x_T)$. This sequence is read by the LSTM module, which outputs its final hidden state $h_T\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$, representing the final clip feature map. A final linear
head $W$ maps $h_T$ to class scores.
\begin{equation}
h_T = \mathrm{LSTM}\big(\phi(x_1),\dots,\phi(x_T)\big),\qquad z = W\,h_T .
\end{equation}
The head \emph{grows} as the model is expanded to new classes. Adding $k$ classes appends $k$ rows to $W$ while keeping the existing rows.

\subsection{The moving freeze boundary}
At each stage a different portion of the model is trained.
Stage~2 (best model selection) keeps the early layers of each pre-trained backbone frozen and fine-tunes only its last block, together with the LSTM and the classifier head.
Stage~3 (continual learning) trains the whole teacher on the new classes, with the classifier head expanded, while adopting strategies to avoid catastrophic forgetting.
Stage~4 (knowledge distillation) freezes the teacher model completely and trains the whole student to match teacher's performance. 
Stage~5 (meta learning) freezes the lower student backbone and meta-trains
only its last four blocks, the LSTM, and the head, so the old features are protected while
the upper layers reshape. 
Within the same stage every tested methododology trains the \emph{same} portion of the model (through different strategies).

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
We refer to \emph{test accuracy} as the fraction of correctly classified clips on the unseen, group-disjoint test set.
In the continual-learning and few-shot stages we further split it by class group: \emph{Old} denotes the classes learned
in earlier tasks, \emph{New} the classes of the most recent task, and \emph{Base} the original ten classes, which measures
long-term retention. When old classes outnumber new ones, a plain average is dominated by the old classes and can reward
a model that learns nothing new. For few-shot learning we therefore also report the harmonic mean
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:harm}
\mathrm{H}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}\,\mathrm{acc}_{\text{old}}}
{\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}+\mathrm{acc}_{\text{old}}},
\end{equation}
which is high only when \emph{both} sides are high~\cite{tao2020fscil}. Finally, to compare models of different
size, we report the number of parameters and, in the backbone stage, the inference time per clip.

\section{Stage 1 --- Data Split and Leakage}
\label{sec:data}
UCF101 natively collects clips into groups. All clips of a group share the same actor, background and camera angle. If one splits data
per clip, segments of the \emph{same} recording can land in train and test, and the model can easily recognise the \emph{scene context} instead of the \emph{action}. We therefore assign each whole
group to a single split (panel S1 of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Source groups shared between splits (20 studied classes, 2{,}679 videos).}
\label{tab:leak}

\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Split & Groups & Groups in $>$1 split & Leak \\
\midrule
Shipped (per clip)        & 500 & 400 & 80\% \\
\textbf{Group-disjoint}   & 500 & 0   & \textbf{0\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Results and interpretation.} As shown in Table~\ref{tab:leak}, the shipped
clip-level split places 400 of the 500 source groups (80\%) in multiple subsets, allowing
closely related clips from the same recording to appear in both training and evaluation
data. By contrast, the group-disjoint split eliminates this overlap while preserving the
standard 72/12/16\% train/validation/test proportions. Consequently, the accuracies
reported in subsequent stages may be lower than results obtained with the shipped split,
but they are not inflated by source-group leakage. Stage~2 measures the size of this
inflation: the same backbones score 4.3 to 36.7 points higher on the shipped split
(Table~\ref{tab:backbone}). Applying this correction at the data
partitioning stage therefore provides a consistent, leakage-free evaluation protocol for
the entire study.

\section{Stage 2 --- Backbone Selection}
\label{sec:backbone}
In this section we evaluate six different backbones in order to select the best one to perform action-recognition on UCF101.
The tested backbones include: a simple CNN trained from scratch, four CNNs pre-trained on ImageNet (ResNet18, DenseNet121,
VGG19-BN, and ResNet50), and a vision transformer, ViT-B/16, also pre-trained on ImageNet.
Each backbone $\phi$ is injected in the following pipeline: video clip $\to\phi\to$ LSTM $\to$ head, as shown in panel S2 of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}; for the ViT, $\phi$ is the 768-d class token of every frame, followed by a two-layer LSTM.
Each resulting architecture is fine-tuned over 10 UCF101 classes under the same training conditions (Adam, learning rate
$10^{-4}$, 5 epochs): the pretrained backbones keep their early layers frozen and fine-tune only their last block (the last
two of the twelve encoder blocks for the ViT), together with the LSTM and the head; the custom CNN is trained entirely.
Every backbone, including the ViT, was trained with the same budget both on UCF101's shipped split, in an earlier
version of the pipeline, and on our group-disjoint split; Table~\ref{tab:backbone} reports both.

\textbf{Results and selection rationale.} On our group-disjoint split (Table~\ref{tab:backbone},
last column), the from-scratch CNN has the largest parameter count (103.1M) yet the lowest test accuracy
(52.8\%). Its size is primarily due to the LSTM receiving a high-dimensional flattened
feature map, illustrating that model capacity alone cannot compensate for the absence of
pretrained spatial features in this limited-data regime. In contrast, all four pretrained
CNNs achieve more than 90\% accuracy.

\emph{The shipped split inflates every result.} On UCF101's shipped split the same models score
4.3 to 36.7 points higher (ResNet50: 98.2\% instead of 93.9\%), and the ranking changes:
DenseNet121 (98.8\%) appears better than ResNet50. The inflation is largest for the
from-scratch CNN (89.5\% instead of 52.8\%): without pretrained features it memorises the scene
context that the leaking test clips share with the training clips. A leaking split can therefore
hide a weak model and reorder the strong ones, as anticipated in Stage~1.

\emph{Vision transformer.} The ViT-B/16 is the most accurate backbone on both splits: 99.4\% on
the shipped split and 94.3\% on the group-disjoint split, where it is inflated by 5.1 points, in line
with the CNNs. On the leakage-free split, however, its lead over ResNet50 amounts to a single test clip
(200 against 199 of the 212 clips correctly classified), well within the variation expected from a
single seed. Most of its errors involve similar jumping actions: 9 of its 12 mistakes are clips
wrongly predicted as high jump, and pole vault is its weakest class (80\% recall).

We select \textbf{ResNet50} because it matches the accuracy of the vision transformer (93.9\% against
94.3\%) with $3.4\times$ fewer parameters (25.9M against 87.4M) and a $3.4\times$ lower inference time
(54.6 against 184.2~ms per clip), and it provides a 2048-dimensional feature representation for the
subsequent stages. Among the CNNs it is also the most accurate, and it requires approximately half the
latency of VGG19-BN (115.7~ms per clip).

\section{Stage 3 --- Continual Learning}
\label{sec:cl}
In this section we aim to extend the knowledge of the selected ResNet50+LSTM model, passing from 10 old classes to 20, increasing the count of 5 new classes every time.
We tested five different methods, that approach the problem of protecting old knowledge in different ways, modifying the base cross-entropy loss of the task using unique strategies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S3.0 Naive}: trains only on the new classes using
$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{new})$, with no explicit mechanism for
preserving knowledge of the old classes.
\item \textbf{S3.1 EWC}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}: adds the penalty
$\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i(\theta_i-\theta_i^{\star})^2$. Here, $F_i$ estimates the
importance of parameter $\theta_i$ to the previous model, and $\theta_i^{\star}$ is its
previous value. The penalty discourages large changes to important parameters.
\item \textbf{S3.2 LwF}~\cite{li2017lwf}: adds the distillation loss
$\lambda\,\mathrm{KD}\big(z_{1:C_{\text{old}}},\,z^{\star}_{1:C_{\text{old}}}\big)$. On
the new-class clips, this loss encourages the current model's predictions for the old
classes to remain close to those produced by a frozen copy of the previous model.
\item \textbf{S3.3 Replay}: stores 15 old clips per class and forms each training batch
with equal numbers of new and replayed clips. The model is trained with
$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{new}\cup\text{old})$.
\item \textbf{S3.4 Replay+LwF}~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}: combines replayed old-class clips
with the LwF distillation loss.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Results and interpretation.}
\emph{S3.0 Naive} achieves high accuracy on each new task (98.2\% on task~1 and 94.9\% on task~2), but
the base-class accuracy falls from 93.9\% to 22.6\% after the first update and to 0.5\%
after the second. Because training batches contain only new-class clips, the shared
parameters are updated without any signal that preserves the old decision boundaries.
\emph{S3.2 LwF} shows a similar pattern: its base accuracy decreases from 26.4\% after
task~1 to 0.0\% after task~2. In the original LwF setting~\cite{li2017lwf}, tasks use
separate output heads and the task identity is known at inference. Our setting is more
demanding because a single growing head must distinguish all classes without knowing the
task. Moreover, the distillation loss is computed only over the old-class outputs on new
clips. It preserves their relative responses but does not directly train the model on old
examples or calibrate old classes against the new ones. Consistent with this limitation,
the confusion matrices show that most forgotten base clips are assigned to new classes.
\emph{S3.1 EWC} provides greater stability, retaining 76.9\% accuracy on the base classes,
but its task~2 accuracy reaches only 36.8\%. The parameter penalty therefore limits
forgetting at the cost of reduced plasticity for learning the newest classes.
\emph{S3.3 Replay} achieves the best final accuracy (89.8\%) and maintains strong
performance across the base classes (89.2\%), task~1 (81.8\%), and task~2 (98.3\%). The
stored clips provide direct supervision for old classes and keep them represented in each
training batch. \emph{S3.4 Replay+LwF} also performs well, but its final accuracy is
slightly lower (87.0\%). In this experiment, adding distillation to replay provides no
additional benefit and reduces old-class retention relative to replay alone.
\textbf{Choice for the pipeline: replay} (panel S3 of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). For this
video stream, replaying a small number of stored clips is more effective than the tested
data-free regularization methods.

\section{Stage 4 --- Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:kd}
In this section we aim to condense the 25.9M-parameter teacher into a smaller device, to be deployed even under computational and architectural constraints.
We chose as student MobileNetV3-Small+LSTM ($\approx$1.8M parameters, $14\times$ smaller). The five
objectives in Fig.~\ref{fig:kd} use the same ImageNet-pretrained student initialization,
training data, computational budget, and random seed. Except for proto-alignment, they use
$\mathcal{L}=0.75\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+0.25\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S4.0 CE only}: sets $\mathcal{L}_{\text{transfer}}=0$ and trains without a
teacher, providing the baseline.
\item \textbf{S4.1 KD}~\cite{hinton2015kd}: matches the softened output distributions of
the teacher and student using
$T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\sigma(z_t/T)\,\|\,\sigma(z_s/T)\big)$, with $T=2$.
\item \textbf{S4.2 Cosine}: aligns the directions of the 256-dimensional clip features,
$1-\cos(h_s,h_t)$.
\item \textbf{S4.3 MSE}~\cite{romero2015fitnets}: uses a learnable regressor $g$ to map
the student feature $h_s$ into the teacher's feature space, then minimizes
$\lVert g(h_s)-h_t\rVert_2^2$. The regressor is removed at inference.
\item \textbf{S4.4 Proto-align} (proposed): before training, the teacher features for each
class are grouped by $k$-means into three \emph{prototypes}. During training, each student
feature is pulled toward the nearest teacher prototype for its class using
$\min_j\lVert h_s-\mu_{y,j}\rVert_2^2$. This term is added to CE and KD:
$\mathcal{L}=0.75\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+0.25\,\mathrm{KD}+0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{proto}}$.
Unlike clip-level feature matching, this objective transfers a class-level summary of the
teacher's representation, following the general idea of prototypical
networks~\cite{snell2017proto}.
\end{itemize}
Because the pretrained student can already learn strong features from class labels, we
also repeat the study with a \emph{reduced training set} of 32 clips per class (640 clips,
approximately one third of the full 1{,}917), while keeping the test set unchanged. The
teacher is the same Stage-3 model trained on the full dataset; it therefore has access to
more training examples than the student, a setting in which knowledge transfer is expected
to be more useful. Fig.~\ref{fig:proto} illustrates proto-alignment in detail.

\textbf{Results and interpretation.} Table~\ref{tab:kd} shows that the best full-data
student is approximately $14\times$ smaller than the teacher and remains within 4.3
percentage points of its accuracy (82.7\% versus 87.0\%). The CE-only baseline is already
strong (80.6\%), partly because the student uses an ImageNet-pretrained backbone.
\emph{S4.1 KD} reaches 79.7\% and therefore provides no improvement over CE alone in this
setting. \emph{S4.2 Cosine} performs best, reaching 82.7\%, or 2.1 points above the
baseline. This objective aligns the directions of the teacher and student clip features
without requiring their magnitudes to match. \emph{S4.3 MSE} is the weakest full-data
method (75.9\%). Matching both the direction and magnitude of features produced by
different backbones may impose a more restrictive target on the student. Because these
results use one seed and 439 test clips, small differences should be interpreted with
caution. The main result of this stage is therefore the compression itself, with cosine
distillation providing the best measured transfer objective. \textbf{Choice for the
pipeline: cosine distillation} (panel S4 of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\textbf{Reduced-data setting.} When training is limited to 32 clips per class, all
full-data methods lose substantial accuracy; for example, the CE baseline decreases from
80.6\% to 61.5\%. Training accuracy still reaches 97--99\%, while test accuracy remains
near 60\%, indicating overfitting. KD (62.0\%) and MSE (61.3\%) perform similarly to CE,
whereas cosine reaches 59.7\%. These objectives transfer a separate teacher target for
each available training clip and provide little improvement when the student sees only a
small set of clips. In contrast, \emph{S4.4 Proto-align} achieves the highest accuracy at
67.9\%: 6.4 points above CE and 5.9 points above KD. It also reduces the gap to the teacher
from 25.3 to 18.9 points. This gain is larger than the approximate binomial standard error
of 2.3 points on 439 test clips, although experiments with additional seeds are needed to
measure training variability.

Proto-alignment differs from the other objectives because it transfers a class-level
summary built from the teacher's full set of features. Pulling each student feature toward
the nearest of three class prototypes encourages samples from the same class to form
compact groups, while multiple prototypes preserve variation in how an action can be
performed. These results suggest that, when training data are scarce, class-level teacher
targets are more useful than clip-level targets. With the full training set, however,
proto-alignment reaches 80.4\%, slightly below CE alone (80.6\%) and 2.3 points below cosine
(82.7\%): when every class has about 96 training clips, the class-level targets add little that
the labels do not already provide. Cosine therefore remains the objective of the full-data
pipeline (panel S4 of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\section{Stage 5 --- Few-Shot Meta-Adaptation}
\label{sec:meta}
\textbf{Design.} Given the 20-class student, how well can it learn \emph{five brand-new
sports} from only a few clips? Every experimental arm uses the same adaptation setup:
8 clips per new sport, 5 epochs, the same learning rate and seed, and a classifier head
expanded from 20 to 25 classes. Thus, only two factors vary (Fig.~\ref{fig:meta}): the
\emph{starting point} and the method used to \emph{protect} the old classes. The three
starting points are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S5.0 Plain}: the distilled student without further preparation.
\item \textbf{S5.1 Fine-tune}: the student after standard supervised training on 40 other
sports. This serves as the control because it uses the same additional data as MAML.
\item \textbf{S5.2 MAML}~\cite{finn2017maml}: the student after first-order MAML training
on the same 40 sports. Each episode samples 5 sports, with 3 support clips and 3 query
clips per sport. The \emph{inner} loop adapts a copy of the model on the support clips,
$\theta'=\theta-\alpha\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}^{\text{sup}}(\theta)$ (5 SGD steps), and the
\emph{outer} loop updates the initialisation using the adapted model's loss on the query clips,
$\theta\leftarrow\theta-\beta\nabla_{\theta'}\mathcal{L}^{\text{qry}}(\theta')$.
\end{itemize}
For S5.1 and S5.2, only the final four backbone blocks, the LSTM, and the classifier head
are trained. A new 20-class head is then fitted to the resulting features through a short
linear-probing stage using 12 clips per class. Each starting point is adapted under three
protection settings: none, replay (8 old clips per class), and replay+LwF. This produces
nine experimental arms, which we rank by the harmonic mean in Eq.~\eqref{eq:harm}.

on new clips rewrites the network, reproducing the catastrophic forgetting observed with
naive continual learning. \emph{MAML alone} achieves the highest new-class accuracy
(0.661) because its initialisation is designed for rapid adaptation and no constraint
limits how far it can move. \emph{Replay} preserves base-class performance in every case
(0.576--0.724), but slows new-class learning, particularly for the plain starting point
(0.295). \emph{MAML+replay} combines a rapidly adaptable initialisation with stored clips
that preserve the old classes, providing the best balance ($H=0.600$). The key comparison
is with the control: \emph{fine-tune+replay} uses the same 40 sports but reaches only
$H=0.530$. The 0.070 improvement therefore comes from meta-learning rather than access to
additional data. The LwF variants perform slightly worse in every case: when only a few
new clips are available, constraining the old-class outputs also restricts learning in the
new classifier rows. Table~\ref{tab:shots} further shows that MAML's advantage lies in its
\emph{initialisation}. The gain is largest with 1 clip (0.26 versus 0.00--0.03) and
disappears by 10 clips (0.65 versus 0.66 for the plain starting point), when sufficient
data and updates allow either model to adapt.
\textbf{Choice for the pipeline: MAML meta-training, adapted with replay} (panel S5 of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\section{Stage 6 --- Few-Shot Learning with a Frozen CLIP Encoder}
\label{sec:clip}
Through Stage~5 we developed a strategy to make our model able to adapt quickly to new tasks. We evaluated also a different approach, borrowing a \emph{foundation model} pre-trained over a vast amount of data and keeping it frozen, using it as a 0-shot feature extractor.
We chose CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} (ViT-B/32), a vision-language model already trained on 400M image--text pairs.
Our goal is to test whether it can learn our actions from a handful of clips. This study
is a reference for Stage~5; it is not part of the pipeline of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\textbf{Architectural design} (Fig.~\ref{fig:clip}). For every video clip, all 16 frames are passed through the frozen CLIP
image encoder, to extract 16 embeddings. These are averaged to obtain one normalised 512-d clip feature map $f$. The task is to learn 10 base classes, across different few-shot scenarios: $k\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ training clips per
class. The model is tested on the 212 clips of the group-disjoint test set.
We compare four different methods:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S6.0 Baseline}: our ResNet50+LSTM model. The ImageNet backbone is frozen,
while the LSTM and classifier head are trained on the $k$ clips per class (Adam,
$10^{-4}$, 10 epochs).
\item \textbf{S6.1 Hybrid prototypes}: the prompt ``a video of a person performing
$\langle$class$\rangle$'' is encoded by the frozen CLIP text encoder to obtain a text
prototype $t_c$. It is combined with the mean CLIP feature $m_c$ of the $k$ training clips
for class $c$ to form the normalised prototype
$p_c=0.4\,t_c+0.6\,m_c$. Each test clip is assigned to the class with the highest cosine
similarity $\cos(f,p_c)$. No parameters are trained; however, because the method uses the
$k$ labelled clips, it is a few-shot method rather than a purely zero-shot classifier.
\item \textbf{S6.2 Linear probe}: a logistic-regression classifier is trained on the
frozen clip features $f$ from the $k$ clips per class, using $C=0.01$.
\item \textbf{S6.3 MLP adapter}: a small MLP is trained on the frozen clip features $f$.
It contains a 512$\to$256 linear layer, batch normalisation, ReLU, dropout of 0.3, and a
classification layer (AdamW, $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, 50 epochs).
\end{itemize}
S6.0 and S6.3 are evaluated for every value of $k$, while S6.1 and S6.2 are evaluated only
for $k=16$.

\textbf{Results and interpretation.} Table~\ref{tab:clip} shows that frozen CLIP features
are effective in the few-shot setting. With one training clip per class, the MLP
adapter reaches 75.5\% accuracy, compared with 17.9\% for ResNet50+LSTM. The comparison is
not fully controlled because the models are trained for different numbers of epochs, but
the large gap indicates the value of CLIP's pretrained representation.

Increasing $k$ from 1 to 4 provides little benefit to the MLP adapter (72.6--75.5\%) because
these clips come from the same recording and contain limited additional variation. Its
accuracy rises to 78.8\% with 8 clips and 88.2\% with 16 clips, when examples from more
recordings are included. This suggests that recording diversity is more useful than adding
highly similar clips.

At $k=16$, the simpler classifiers perform best: hybrid prototypes reach 90.1\% and the
linear probe reaches 89.6\%, compared with 88.2\% for the MLP adapter. Both are also close
to the 93.9\% accuracy of the Stage-2 model trained on the full dataset. These results show
that strong frozen features require only a lightweight classifier; with 160 training
clips, the additional capacity of the MLP does not improve generalisation.

\textbf{Comparison between MAML (Stage 5) and CLIP (Stage 6).} Both approaches address the same
problem learning a new class from a few clips but in opposite ways, and each is preferable under
different criteria:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Prior knowledge.} CLIP relies on representations learned from 400M web image--text pairs,
which explains why a single clip per class already yields 75.5\% accuracy. MAML, in contrast, only knows
the UCF101 sports on which it was meta-trained, and still has to update the network to learn a new class.
\item \emph{Forgetting.} With frozen CLIP features, adding a class only requires adding a prototype or a
row of a small classifier; the old classes are left untouched, so nothing is forgotten and no replay
buffer is required. MAML updates the network and therefore needs replay to preserve the old classes.
\item \emph{Model size.} The frozen CLIP image encoder has approximately 88M parameters, about $50\times$
more than the 1.8M-parameter distilled student and more than the 25.9M-parameter teacher. It therefore
gives up the compression obtained in Stage~4.
\item \emph{Temporal modelling.} Averaging frame features discards their order, whereas our recogniser uses
an LSTM. The 10 base classes can largely be distinguished from the scene and objects of a single frame
(e.g., a horse, a pommel horse, or a tabla), which favours CLIP; actions that differ mainly in their motion
would test this limitation.
\end{itemize}
The two studies are not evaluated on the same task: Stage~5 adds 5 new sports to 20 known classes and is
scored with the harmonic mean, whereas Stage~6 learns 10 classes from scratch. Their results therefore
cannot be compared directly. A like-for-like comparison would require evaluating the CLIP classifiers
(S6.1 and S6.3) on the YouTube test of Section~\ref{sec:ulal}, next to the MAML student; we leave this
evaluation to future work.

\section{The ULAL Pipeline on Real Video}
\label{sec:ulal}
\textbf{Final pipeline} 
In this section we aim to assemble the final pipeline, selecting the best performing strategy for each stage (backbone selection, continual learning, knowledge distillation, few-shot meta learning adaptation).
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates such pipeline, which executes the following protocol, over a $10\!\to\!20\!\to\!30$ classes stream:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{S1 Data preparation}: all video clips are pre-processed and splitted into train/val/test avoiding data leakage.
\item \textbf{S2 Teacher}: the ResNet50+LSTM teacher is trained on 10 base classes.
\item \textbf{S3 CL with replay strategy}: the teacher's head grows to adapt sequentially to 20 and then 30 classes; every training batch
mixes new clips with few stored old clips (15 per class) to prevent forgetting.
\item \textbf{S4 Cosine distillation}: the frozen 30-class teacher is distilled into the
MobileNet student with CE + cosine strategy.
\item \textbf{S5 MAML}: the student is meta-trained with first-order MAML on 35 additional sports, i.e., the
40 meta-training sports of Stage~5 excluding the 5 that are already among the 30 known classes. Episodes are
5-way 5-shot, and only the last four backbone blocks, the LSTM, and the head are trained. Each meta-training
clip is taken from a different source video, so the query clips always come from videos other than those of
the support clips. The 30-class head is then re-fitted on 12 clips per class.
\item \textbf{Real-video test}: we collect YouTube videos of five sports that are unseen
by all models: golf swing, front crawl, hammer throw, handstand push-ups, and handstand
walking. For each sport, we use two videos for adaptation and reserve two separate videos
for testing, with each video divided into 10 clips. One front-crawl video was unavailable,
so that class is learned from a single source video. Each model expands its classifier
from 30 to 35 classes and adapts on the same 10 clips per new sport, together with a replay
buffer containing eight old clips per class. Adaptation uses 1, 2, 3, or 5 passes over the
training clips, corresponding to 13, 26, 39, or 65 updates. The smaller budgets test the
rapid adaptation for which MAML is designed. We compare the MAML student with the teacher
and the plain distilled student under the same data, seed, and update budget. We report
accuracy on the unseen YouTube test videos (new classes), retained accuracy on the 30 UCF
classes (old classes), and the harmonic mean of the two.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Results on UCF101.} Table~\ref{tab:ulal} shows that replay remains effective on
the longer stream. After two increments of 10 classes, the teacher reaches 79.7\% accuracy
over all 30 classes, with similar performance on the old (78.8\%) and new classes (81.4\%).
The distilled student reaches 67.2\% with approximately $14\times$ fewer parameters. This
gap is larger than in Stage~4, although the experiments are not directly comparable because
the stream contains more classes and uses fewer training epochs. After meta-training and
re-fitting the classifier on 12 clips per class, the MAML student reaches 60.8\%. Its
6.4-point reduction relative to the distilled student reflects the trade-off between
retaining performance on known classes and preparing the model for rapid adaptation.

\textbf{Results on real video.} Table~\ref{tab:youtube} shows that the MAML student adapts
faster than the plain distilled student under small update budgets. After one pass (13
updates), their new-class accuracies are 0.16 and 0.00, respectively. After two passes (26
updates), the MAML student reaches 0.36, compared with 0.23 for the plain student and 0.37
for the much larger teacher. This result is consistent with the purpose of MAML: providing
an initialization that can adapt in a small number of updates.

With more updates, the advantage decreases. After three passes, all models obtain
new-class accuracies between 0.42 and 0.49. After five passes, the plain student performs
best on both the new sports (0.57) and the harmonic mean ($H=0.578$), while the MAML student
remains at 0.47 on the new sports. This pattern is consistent with Stage~5
(Table~\ref{tab:shots}): meta-learning improves adaptation speed, but does not necessarily
produce the highest accuracy after longer training.

\emph{Retention of old classes.} Replay limits forgetting for all three models. Across
the four update budgets, old-class accuracy ranges from 0.744 to 0.776 for the teacher,
0.583 to 0.644 for the distilled student, and 0.533 to 0.545 for the MAML student. The MAML
student remains lower because its initial 30-class accuracy is also lower (0.608;
Table~\ref{tab:ulal}). Nevertheless, its harmonic mean exceeds that of the plain student
after one and two passes, is equal after three passes, and is lower after five.

\emph{Teacher.} The teacher retains the highest old-class accuracy and achieves the best
harmonic mean through three passes ($H=0.591$). Its new-class accuracy then decreases from
0.49 to 0.35 after five passes, while its old-class accuracy remains stable. This may
indicate overfitting to the small adaptation set, but the limited test size prevents a
firm conclusion.

These results have three important limitations. First, the real-video test contains only
10 videos (100 clips), so a single video can change new-class accuracy by as much as 0.10.
Second, one front-crawl video was unavailable, leaving only one adaptation video for that
class. Third, some classes exhibit a domain shift between adaptation and test videos; for
example, the handstand push-up videos change from a studio to a gym setting.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Four themes recur across the pipeline. First, \emph{the evaluation protocol matters}: without the
group-disjoint split, every accuracy would be inflated by scene memorisation (by 4.3 to 36.7 points in
Stage~2), and without the harmonic mean the few-shot ranking would reward models that learn nothing new.
Second, \emph{stored data is the most effective protection against forgetting}: on video, rehearsal of a
few clips per class outperforms every data-free method (EWC, LwF). LwF in particular was designed for a
setting in which the task is known at test time, and it collapses when a single head must choose among all
classes. Third, \emph{each technique is useful in a specific regime}: the main benefit of distillation is
compression, and with scarce data the teacher helps most through class-level prototypes rather than
per-clip targets; meta-learning is most useful when new classes must be learned from very few clips and in
very few updates, while with more clips or more updates the plain student catches up, both in Stage~5 and on
real video. Fourth, \emph{prior knowledge and fast adaptation answer different constraints}: frozen CLIP
features learn the base classes from a single clip (75.5\%), but the CLIP encoder is $50\times$ larger than the
student and therefore suits deployments without size constraints, whereas the MAML student keeps the
1.8M-parameter budget targeted by ULAL and learns new classes within a few updates.

\textbf{Limitations.} All results are obtained with a single seed and on a single dataset, with short class
streams (up to 30 classes). Rehearsal stores real clips, so its memory cost grows with the number of
classes. The real-video test is small
(5 sports, 10 test videos) and depends on downloadable YouTube videos (one of the 20 could not be
downloaded); a larger set of videos and several seeds would be required to make the differences observed
after three and five passes conclusive.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
In this work we presented ULAL, a compact real-video pipeline for lifelong action recognition that runs on
a single free GPU. A leakage-free, group-disjoint split makes the reported results trustworthy, and a
ResNet50+LSTM provides a strong teacher (93.9\%), as accurate as a vision transformer $3.4\times$ its size.
Among the continual-learning strategies, rehearsal is the
most effective (89.8\%, against 26.0\% for naive fine-tuning). Cosine distillation produces a $14\times$
smaller student that retains 82.7\% accuracy, while with scarce data the proposed prototype-alignment
objective is the most effective way to use the teacher (+6.4 points over cross-entropy). Evaluated with a
harmonic metric and a matched fine-tune control, MAML combined with replay is the best-balanced few-shot
adapter ($H=0.600$). As a reference, a frozen CLIP encoder learns the base classes from a single clip
(75.5\%, and 90.1\% with 16 clips and no training), which makes it attractive when model size is not a
constraint. When the selected methods are combined into a single pipeline and tested on unseen YouTube
videos, the meta-trained student is the faster of the two students: after 26 updates it matches the
$14\times$ larger teacher on the new sports (0.36 against 0.37) and outperforms the plain student (0.23),
which catches up only with more updates. Overall, each selected method contributes the capability for which
it was chosen.
