\begin{abstract}
We present \emph{Unified Lifelong Action Learning} (ULAL), an end-to-end pipeline that
studies how a video action recogniser can keep learning new actions over time without
forgetting the old ones, trained directly on real video frames (no cached features).
The pipeline (i) builds an honest, group-disjoint split that removes an 80\% leakage in
UCF101's shipped split; (ii) selects a ResNet50+LSTM backbone from a five-way bake-off
(93.9\% on the base classes); (iii) extends it to new classes with five
continual-learning methods, where rehearsal reaches 89.8\% after the full stream while
plain fine-tuning collapses to 26.0\%; (iv) compresses the best model into a
MobileNetV3-Small student that keeps 82.7\% at $\sim$14$\times$ fewer parameters; and
(v) adapts the student to brand-new, few-shot classes with first-order MAML, where
\emph{MAML+replay} reaches the best balance (harmonic mean 0.60) and beats a matched
fine-tune baseline, isolating the meta-learning effect. For every stage we explain
\emph{why} the methods rank as they do from their abilities. A final capstone notebook
(ULAL) chains the best choice of each stage and is described here, pending its run.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
continual learning, catastrophic forgetting, action recognition, knowledge
distillation, meta-learning, few-shot learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Deep video models forget. Fine-tuning a trained recogniser on new action classes
collapses its accuracy on the old ones---\emph{catastrophic
forgetting}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc}. Lifelong (continual) learning aims to add
classes over time while keeping what was already learned. Video makes this harder than
images: clips are large and temporal, so storing and replaying past data is expensive.

This paper describes one reproducible pipeline on UCF101~\cite{soomro2012ucf101},
trained \emph{directly on real video frames}, so every number reflects the deployed
model. Each stage is one notebook and answers one question: \emph{how do we measure
honestly? which backbone? how do we stop forgetting? how do we shrink the model? and
how do we learn brand-new classes from only a handful of clips?} Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An honest split.} We show 80\% of UCF101 groups leak across the shipped
splits and replace them with a group-disjoint split.
\item \textbf{A controlled study} of five continual-learning methods, four distillation
objectives, and a nine-arm meta-learning comparison whose fine-tune control isolates
the meta-learning effect.
\item \textbf{Explanations, not just tables.} For every stage we first say \emph{why}
one method should beat another from its ability, then show the measured result.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Continual learning.} EWC~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} penalises changes to weights
important for old tasks; LwF~\cite{li2017lwf} keeps old-class outputs stable using the
model's own past predictions; rehearsal replays a few stored exemplars, and
iCaRL~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl} combines replay with distillation. We evaluate all of
these on video. \textbf{Knowledge distillation.} A compact student can match a larger
teacher's softened logits~\cite{hinton2015kd} or its features; we compare logit and
feature (cosine, MSE) objectives. \textbf{Meta/few-shot learning.}
MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} learns an initialisation that adapts quickly from few
examples; because base classes outnumber new ones, we report the harmonic mean of base
and new accuracy, the standard few-shot class-incremental metric.

\section{Method, Model, and Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
The recogniser applies a CNN backbone to each of a clip's 16 frames, runs an LSTM over
those frame features, and classifies from the final LSTM state. The classifier
\emph{grows} as new classes arrive (new rows appended, old rows kept), and only the
last backbone block is fine-tuned. Clips are 16 frames at $224\times224$, ImageNet
normalised, on a single Colab T4 GPU. The group-disjoint split uses 18/3/4 groups per
class. Within each stage, every compared method shares the settings in
Table~\ref{tab:hparams}, so any difference in the result comes only from the mechanism
under test---that is what makes each comparison fair. Seeds are fixed and reset before
every training run.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Main hyperparameters per stage (shared across the methods compared in it).}
\label{tab:hparams}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Stage & Key settings \\
\midrule
Backbone (2)     & Adam, lr $10^{-4}$, 5 epochs \\
Continual (3)    & Adam, lr $10^{-4}$, 5 epochs/task; replay 15/class; \\
                 & LwF weight 3.0, $T{=}5$; EWC $\lambda{=}2800$ \\
Distillation (4) & Adam, lr $10^{-4}$, 15 epochs; $T{=}2$, CE/KD $0.75/0.25$ \\
Meta-train (5)   & 5-way 3-shot, 60 episodes $\times$ 8 epochs, \\
                 & inner SGD 5 steps @ $0.05$, meta-lr $10^{-4}$ \\
Adaptation (5)   & 8 clips/class, 5 epochs, lr $3\!\times\!10^{-4}$; \\
                 & replay 8/class; LwF $0.5$, $T{=}2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{How to read the numbers.} \emph{Test accuracy} is the fraction correct on the
unseen (group-disjoint) test set. \emph{Old/New accuracy} split the score into classes
learned earlier versus the newest task. For the few-shot stage, where 20 old classes
outnumber 5 new ones, a plain average is dominated by the old classes and rewards a
method that simply guards them; we therefore add the \emph{balanced} mean
$(\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}+\mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}})/2$ and the \emph{harmonic} mean,
which weight new and old equally and, for the harmonic, punish sacrificing either side.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 1 --- Data Split and Leakage}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Why this matters first.} A UCF101 file \texttt{v\_Class\_gXX\_cYY.avi} encodes a
\emph{group} \texttt{gXX} (one source video) and a \emph{clip} \texttt{cYY} (one segment
of it). All clips of a group are near-duplicate frames. If the split is made per clip,
segments of the same recording land in both train and test, and the model is scored on
footage it trained on---it can recognise the \emph{scene} instead of the \emph{action}
and look far better than it is. We must therefore fix the split before trusting any
later number, by assigning each whole group to a single split.

\textbf{Result.} Across the 20 studied classes (2{,}679 videos, 500 groups), the
shipped split leaks \textbf{400 of 500 groups (80\%)} across train/val/test; the
group-disjoint split leaks \textbf{0} (Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage}). The disjoint split keeps
the standard $\approx$72/12/16\% train/val/test proportions.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\figph{leakage.png}{[leakage.png] shipped vs group-disjoint leakage bar.}
\caption{Groups (source videos) that leak across splits: 80\% in the shipped split
versus 0\% in the group-disjoint split. Every later number is read against the clean
split, so the accuracies are lower but honest.}
\label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\section{Stage 2 --- Backbone Selection}
\label{sec:backbone}
\textbf{Why we expect differences.} The backbone supplies the per-frame features; the
LSTM only has to combine them over time. A network with no prior knowledge (a
from-scratch CNN) must learn good spatial features from a few thousand clips and will
struggle, whereas ImageNet-pretrained backbones already carry rich features and should
cluster high. Among the pretrained ones the choice is a trade-off of accuracy, feature
size, and speed, so we compare five and pick the best balance to be the teacher.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:backbone} gives the measured numbers. The from-scratch
CNN is far behind (52.8\%); all pretrained backbones exceed 90\%. \textbf{ResNet50 is
both the most accurate (93.9\%) and a good size/speed balance}, so it is chosen as the
teacher for every later stage.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Backbone bake-off on the 10 base classes (group-disjoint test).}
\label{tab:backbone}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Backbone & Test acc.\ (\%) & Params (M) & ms/clip \\
\midrule
ScratchCNN        & 52.8 & 103.1 & 10.2 \\
ResNet18          & 90.1 & 12.0 & 15.7 \\
DenseNet121       & 92.0 & 8.3  & 53.1 \\
VGG19-BN          & 92.0 & 20.8 & 115.7 \\
\textbf{ResNet50 (teacher)} & \textbf{93.9} & 25.9 & 54.6 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why the ordering.} The from-scratch CNN has no prior features \emph{and} its
LSTM ingests a huge flattened feature map, giving it the most parameters (103M) yet the
worst accuracy---capacity without useful priors does not help on little data. The
pretrained backbones all reach the low-90s because the hard part (spatial features) is
already solved. ResNet50 edges the others thanks to its deeper, richer 2048-d feature,
which also gives the later distillation and meta stages a clean feature space to build
on; its speed (55 ms/clip) is acceptable and far better than VGG19-BN (116 ms).

\section{Stage 3 --- Continual Learning}
\label{sec:cl}
\textbf{The five methods and why they should differ.} Starting from the base teacher we
grow the head and train on a $10\!\to\!15\!\to\!20$ class stream with each method. Their
\emph{abilities} predict the outcome: \emph{Naive} (plain fine-tuning) has no way to
protect the past, so it should forget badly; \emph{EWC}~\cite{kirkpatrick2017ewc} can
only slow weight change with a penalty (no stored data), so it should protect some but
not learn new classes freely; \emph{LwF}~\cite{li2017lwf} keeps old \emph{outputs}
stable through the model's own past predictions (no stored data), a stronger signal
than a weight penalty but still drifting; \emph{Replay} re-exposes the model to a few
real old clips, which should directly refresh the old classes; and
\emph{Replay+LwF}~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl} adds output-stability on top of re-exposure.
All arms share the schedule in Table~\ref{tab:hparams}; only the mechanism differs.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:cl} reports accuracy after the \emph{full} 20-class
stream, split into old classes (0--14) and the newest task (15--19), plus the base
(0--9) retention. Figure~\ref{fig:forget} contrasts naive forgetting with replay.

Method & Final & Old (0--14) & New (15--19) & Base (0--9) \\
\midrule

LwF               & 30.5 & 5.3  & 100.0 & 0.0 \\
EWC               & 62.0 & 71.1 & 36.8 & 76.9 \\
Replay+LwF        & 87.0 & 82.6 & 99.2 & 83.0 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why the winners win.} The table follows exactly from the abilities.
\emph{Naive} and \emph{LwF} learn the newest task almost perfectly (94.9\% and 100\%)
but erase everything older (old $\le$5\%): with the shared weights free to move and no
old data, the network re-purposes itself entirely for the last task. LwF even learns
the new task better than naive (its distillation stabilises training) yet still forgets,
because keeping \emph{outputs} stable cannot substitute for \emph{seeing} old data.
\emph{EWC} is the opposite failure: its penalty protects the old classes (base 76.9\%)
but is so restrictive that the new task barely learns (36.8\%)---a stability/plasticity
lock. \emph{Replay} and \emph{Replay+LwF} win decisively (89.8\% and 87.0\%) because
re-exposing even 15 clips per class refreshes the old decision boundaries \emph{while}
the new task is learned. Notably plain \emph{Replay} slightly beats Replay+LwF here: the
buffer already protects the old classes, so the extra LwF distillation adds a mild drag
on new-class learning without a retention gain. The lesson: \emph{on video, storing a
little data beats any data-free regulariser.}

\section{Stage 4 --- Knowledge Distillation}
\label{sec:kd}
\textbf{Why and how.} The best continual model is a 25.9M-parameter ResNet50 teacher;
for deployment we want something far smaller. We distil it into a
MobileNetV3-Small+LSTM student ($\sim$1.8M parameters, $\sim$14$\times$ smaller) and
compare four objectives: cross-entropy only (CE), CE+KD on softened
logits~\cite{hinton2015kd}, and CE with a cosine or a regressor-MSE loss between the
256-d LSTM states of teacher and student. We expect the student to recover most of the
teacher's accuracy (its knowledge transfers) but the gap between objectives to be small,
because the student backbone is itself pretrained and already strong.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:kd}: the student keeps \textbf{82.7\%} of the teacher's
87.0\% at 14$\times$ fewer parameters, with cosine feature-distillation the best of the
four objectives.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Distillation into the MobileNetV3-Small student (20-class test).}
\label{tab:kd}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Model & Test acc.\ (\%) & Params (M) \\
\midrule
Teacher (Replay+LwF, ResNet50) & 87.0 & 25.9 \\
Student, CE only               & 80.6 & 1.8 \\
Student, CE + KD               & 79.7 & 1.8 \\
\textbf{Student, CE + cosine}  & \textbf{82.7} & 1.8 \\
Student, CE + MSE (regressor)  & 75.9 & 1.8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why.} Compression works because the teacher's decision function transfers: a
14$\times$ smaller student reaches within $\sim$4 points of the teacher. Among
objectives, \emph{cosine} on the LSTM state wins because it aligns the \emph{direction}
of the student's temporal feature with the teacher's without forcing its exact
magnitude, which is a gentle, achievable target across two different backbones. Plain
\emph{KD} on logits helps less here---the student is pretrained, so labels already get
it far and the extra ``dark knowledge'' is small. \emph{MSE} is harshest (it demands
matching magnitude too) and does worst. The honest headline is \emph{compression}
(teacher-level behaviour at 14$\times$ fewer parameters), with a modest bonus from
feature-cosine distillation.

\section{Stage 5 --- Few-Shot Meta-Adaptation}
\label{sec:meta}
\textbf{The question and the design.} Given the deployed 20-class student, how well can
it learn \emph{brand-new} classes from only a handful of clips? We keep the 20 base
classes and add 5 new sports (a 5-way task, matching the 5-way meta-training). Every
method uses the \emph{same} few-shot adaptation---same 8 support clips per class, same 5
epochs, same learning rate, same seed, same replay buffer/LwF teacher---so only two
things vary: the \emph{initialisation} and whether replay/LwF are on. Three inits
(\emph{plain} student; \emph{fine-tune}, i.e.\ the student after ordinary training on 30
auxiliary sports; and \emph{MAML}, the student after meta-training on the same 30
sports) are crossed with three protection settings, giving nine arms. The
\emph{fine-tune} arm is the key control: it saw the same extra data as MAML but trained
in the ordinary way, so \emph{MAML vs fine-tune} isolates meta-learning from ``just
more data.''

\textbf{Why the metric.} With 20 base and 5 new classes, a plain overall accuracy is
$20/25$ base, so it rewards a method that guards the base and learns nothing new. We
rank by the harmonic mean of base and new accuracy,
\begin{equation}
\text{harmonic}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}\,\mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}}}
{\mathrm{acc}_{\text{new}}+\mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}}},
\end{equation}
which weights the two equally and collapses to zero if either is zero.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:meta} (measured; base before adapting $=0.759$) ranks
the nine arms. \textbf{MAML+replay is best}, reaching a balanced \textbf{0.60}, and it
\emph{beats fine-tune+replay by 0.07 harmonic}---so the gain is really from
meta-learning, not merely the extra data. Figures~\ref{fig:metacompare}--\ref{fig:oldnew}
show the comparison, the shot-count trend, the meta-training curve, and a readable
old-vs-new confusion.

the way, erasing the old classes. \emph{Replay alone} is the opposite (base 0.677, new
0.295): re-exposure protects the old classes but the un-primed init learns the new
sports slowly. \emph{MAML+replay} combines both abilities---a fast-adapting init
\emph{and} re-exposure---so it is best balanced (0.60). The \emph{fine-tune} control saw
the same 30 sports, so \emph{fine-tune+replay} is a strong baseline (0.53), but
MAML+replay still beats it by 0.07, which is the clean evidence that \emph{meta-learning
itself}, not just the extra data, is responsible. The \emph{LwF} variants over-protect:
with only a few new clips, distilling the old outputs crushes the fresh new-class head,
so their new accuracy is lower. Finally, the shot sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}) shows the
meta advantage is a property of the \emph{init}: it is largest at 1 clip and shrinks as
data grows---so MAML \emph{complements} rehearsal rather than replacing it.

\section{The ULAL Capstone (to be run)}
\label{sec:ulal}
The final notebook, \texttt{ULAL.ipynb}, chains the best choice of every stage into one
lifelong pipeline and is evaluated on real video. \emph{This section describes the
intended run; the numbers are expectations, not measurements.}

\textbf{Stage by stage.} (1) Train the ResNet50+LSTM teacher on the base classes
(Stage~2 winner). (2) Grow it across the class stream with \emph{replay}, the winning
retention ability from Stage~3. (3) Distil the grown teacher into the MobileNet student
(Stage~4), keeping most of the accuracy at $\sim$14$\times$ fewer parameters for
deployment. (4) Meta-train the compact student and then adapt it to brand-new classes
from a handful of clips (Stage~5). (5) Test on real clips: for \emph{known} classes this
measures domain robustness (test the student directly); for \emph{new} classes it
measures learn-from-few (expand the head and few-shot adapt, expecting accuracy to rise
from near zero after only a few examples).

\textbf{Why this order.} Each stage supplies an ability the next needs---strong
features, data-rich retention, deployable size, fast few-shot extension. The capstone is
the claim that these abilities \emph{compose} into one model that is accurate, small,
retains old classes, and still picks up new ones from little data. We expect the student
to keep most of its known-class accuracy on real clips and to improve markedly on new
classes after few-shot adaptation, mirroring Stage~5. The measured numbers will be added
once the run completes.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Three themes recur. \emph{Measurement matters:} the group-disjoint split lowers but
trusts every number, and in Stage~5 the harmonic mean reveals MAML+replay's win that a
base-dominated average hides. \emph{Data beats cleverness for retention:} on video,
rehearsal of a few clips outperforms every data-free regulariser (EWC, LwF).
\emph{Each tool has a regime:} distillation's win is compression, and meta-learning's
win is few-shot extension---asked outside their regime (e.g.\ MAML with plenty of data)
they tie simpler baselines.

Limitations: a single dataset and short streams (up to $20{+}5$ classes); rehearsal
stores clips, so its retention is not free (generative rehearsal is future work);
meta-learning's advantage is confined to the few-shot regime; the small student caps the
achievable new-class accuracy; and the capstone's real-video test depends on
downloadable clips and is still pending.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
ULAL is a compact, honest, real-video pipeline for lifelong action recognition. A
leakage-free split makes the numbers trustworthy; ResNet50+LSTM is a strong teacher
(93.9\%); rehearsal is the strongest continual learner (89.8\%); distillation yields a
14$\times$ smaller student at 82.7\%; and, scored fairly, MAML+replay is the
best-balanced few-shot adapter (0.60 harmonic), with a fine-tune control confirming the
gain is from meta-learning. The capstone composes the best of each stage and is left to
a final run.
